Ever read "jasmine, peach and black tea" on a bag of coffee and wondered whether you would ever taste any of that? You can. Tasting coffee is not a talent reserved for professionals; it is a skill built through attention, comparison and vocabulary. The professional method for evaluating coffee, called cupping, is surprisingly easy to adapt at home, and it is the fastest way to train your palate. This guide shows you how to set it up, what to focus on, and how to describe what you find.

Why Cupping Works

When you brew with different methods and recipes, you are tasting your technique as much as the coffee. Cupping removes those variables. Every sample gets the same dose, grind, water and timing, and the coffee is steeped directly in the bowl, so differences in the cup come from the beans. Tasting two or more coffees side by side also makes contrasts obvious that you would miss tasting one coffee alone. A Kenyan next to a Brazilian will teach you what "acidity" means faster than any definition.

What You Need

  • Two to four different coffees, ideally roasted within the past few weeks.
  • Identical cups or bowls of about 180–250 ml capacity, one or two per coffee.
  • A grinder, a coffee scale and a kettle.
  • Deep soup spoons, a cup of hot water to rinse them, and a spittoon cup if you are tasting many samples.
  • Clean, good-tasting water; see our guide to water for coffee.
  • A notebook or score sheet.

A Home Cupping Protocol

This simplified protocol follows the general structure used by coffee professionals, scaled for a home kitchen.

  1. Weigh and grind. Weigh 8.25 g of whole beans per 150 ml of water you will use. Grind each sample separately, medium-coarse. Purge the grinder with a few beans from the next coffee between samples.
  2. Smell the dry fragrance. Within a few minutes of grinding, lift each cup to your nose and inhale. Note anything that comes to mind: nutty, floral, fruity, spicy, sugary.
  3. Pour. Heat water to about 93°C (200°F) and fill each cup to the brim, making sure all grounds are wet. Start a timer; our brew timer works well.
  4. Break the crust. At about 4 minutes, a crust of grounds will have formed on top. Put your nose close to the cup and push through the crust with a spoon, stirring gently back toward you three times. Inhale the burst of aroma. Rinse the spoon between cups.
  5. Skim. Use two spoons to lift off the foam and floating grounds. Most remaining grounds will sink.
  6. Slurp. Once the coffee has cooled for a few minutes, take a spoonful and slurp it forcefully so it sprays across your palate. Move between cups, tasting each one.
  7. Taste as it cools. Revisit every cup every few minutes until it reaches room temperature. Flavors shift dramatically: sweetness and acidity often become clearer as coffee cools, and defects become more obvious.

Pro tip: Taste blind when you can. Ask someone to label the cups underneath with numbers, so the origin on the bag does not steer your perception.

The Attributes to Judge

Professional cupping forms score coffee on several attributes. You do not need to score numerically at home, but thinking through each attribute helps you taste deliberately.

AttributeWhat to ask yourself
Fragrance and aromaWhat do the dry grounds and the wet crust smell like? How intense is it?
FlavorWhat tastes and aromas combine in the mouth? Are they distinct or muddled?
AcidityIs there brightness? Is it crisp like green apple, juicy like citrus, or soft like stone fruit?
SweetnessIs there a sense of sugar, honey, caramel or ripe fruit?
BodyHow heavy or textured does it feel? Thin like tea, round like milk, syrupy?
AftertasteWhat lingers after you swallow or spit, and for how long? Is it pleasant or dry?
BalanceDo all the elements fit together, or does one dominate?
Clean cupAre there off-flavors such as moldy, papery, rubbery or ferment that should not be there?

Building Your Flavor Vocabulary

Most of what we call flavor is aroma, detected through the back of the nose while we taste. The tongue itself contributes sweet, sour, salty, bitter and savory. Descriptors on coffee bags are attempts to connect those aromas to familiar foods.

Start broad and narrow down. First ask which large family a flavor belongs to, such as fruity, floral, nutty, chocolatey, spicy, roasted or sweet. Then ask which branch: is the fruit citrus, berry, stone fruit or tropical? Then go specific: lemon, orange or grapefruit? Our interactive flavor wheel is built for exactly this process. The professional version of the coffee taster's flavor wheel is published by the Specialty Coffee Association.

Some common pairings to look for:

  • Washed East African coffees: citrus, berries, black tea, florals.
  • Natural-processed coffees: ripe strawberry, blueberry, wine-like or tropical notes.
  • Brazil and many Central Americans: chocolate, nuts, caramel, brown sugar.
  • Darker roasts: roasted, smoky, bittersweet chocolate, molasses.

Exercises to Train Your Palate

  • The triangle test. Prepare three cups, two of one coffee and one of another. Try to identify the odd one out. It sharpens your ability to detect difference before you can name it.
  • Process comparison. Cup a washed and a natural coffee from the same country. The contrast teaches you how processing shapes flavor; see our processing methods guide.
  • Kitchen references. Smell and taste real lemons, dried apricots, roasted almonds, dark chocolate and black tea before a session. Your brain needs reference memories to draw on.
  • Temperature tracking. Write notes at hot, warm and room temperature for the same cup.

Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Do not taste right after strong food, mint, coffee with sugar or alcohol. Avoid fragrances in the room. Do not judge a coffee only when it is very hot, when heat masks nuance. And do not worry about being wrong. Two experienced tasters often describe the same coffee differently, and your own notes only need to be useful to you. If a term trips you up, look it up in our coffee glossary. With a few sessions, you will start finding those jasmine and peach notes on your own.
