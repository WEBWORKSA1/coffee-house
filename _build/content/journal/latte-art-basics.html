A glossy heart on top of a cappuccino looks like magic, but it rests on a few physical principles anyone can learn. Latte art is the visible result of two things working together: well-textured milk and controlled pouring. Get the milk right and you have done most of the work. This guide covers the setup, the steaming, the pour itself and three foundation patterns, with a troubleshooting section for when things go sideways.

What You Need

You need an espresso machine with a steam wand, a stainless steel milk pitcher with a defined spout, cold milk and a wide cup. A pitcher with a sharper spout gives finer lines; a rounder spout is more forgiving for hearts. Cup choice matters more than many beginners realize: a wide, shallow bowl of about 180–300 ml gives you room to work, while a narrow mug makes patterns cramped. If you are still putting together your setup, see our espresso machine buying guide for what to look for in steam power.

Start with good espresso. A shot with stable, reddish-brown crema provides a canvas that contrasts with white foam. Thin or pale crema makes patterns look muddy. Our espresso at home guide covers dialing in.

Steaming Silky Microfoam

Microfoam is milk filled with bubbles so tiny you cannot see them individually. It looks like wet paint and pours in a smooth stream. Large bubbles or stiff, dry foam will not draw.

  1. Fill and chill. Fill the pitcher to just below the base of the spout, roughly one-third full. Cold milk gives you more time to texture.
  2. Purge the wand. Open the steam briefly to clear condensed water.
  3. Stretch. Place the tip just below the surface, slightly off-center, and open the steam fully. You should hear a gentle, paper-tearing hiss. Let the milk volume grow by about 20–30% for a latte, a little more for a cappuccino.
  4. Roll. Raise the pitcher slightly so the tip sinks a little deeper. The milk should spin in a whirlpool, folding bubbles in and breaking them down.
  5. Stop at temperature. Close the steam when the pitcher is just too hot to hold comfortably, about 60–65°C (140–149°F). Above roughly 70°C (158°F), milk loses sweetness and foam stability.
  6. Polish. Tap the pitcher once on the counter to pop surface bubbles, then swirl until the milk is glossy, with no separation between foam and liquid.

Pro tip: Practice texture first, patterns second. Steam water with a drop of dish soap to practice the hiss and the whirlpool without wasting milk, then rinse everything thoroughly.

The Pour: Height, Flow and Position

Every pattern uses the same three controls.

  • Height. Pouring from high, about 8–10 cm (3–4 in) above the cup, sends milk under the crema without marking the surface. Pouring from low, with the spout almost touching the surface, lets white foam float and draw.
  • Flow. A thin stream sinks; a thicker stream carries foam. Patterns usually need a confident, medium-thick flow once you drop low.
  • Position. Where the milk lands determines where the pattern forms. Tilting the cup toward the pitcher brings the surface closer and helps patterns spread.

Every pour follows two phases. First, the base: tilt the cup about 45 degrees and pour from height into the center to mix milk and espresso until the cup is about half full. Second, the design: bring the spout close, increase flow slightly, and level the cup as it fills.

Pattern One: The Heart

The heart is the foundation of almost everything else.

  1. Pour the base. Fill halfway from height, aiming at the center.
  2. Drop and hold. Bring the spout right down to the surface in the center. A white circle will appear and grow.
  3. Let it grow. Keep a steady flow as you level the cup. The circle should expand to about two-thirds of the surface.
  4. Lift and cut. Raise the pitcher slightly, reduce flow to a thin stream, and draw straight through the circle toward the far edge. The cut pulls the circle into a heart.

Pattern Two: The Tulip

A tulip stacks several small hearts. Pour the base, then drop low and push out a small blob of foam, stop the flow briefly, and push a second blob just behind the first so it nudges it forward. Repeat for three or more layers, then lift and cut through all of them. The key skill is starting and stopping the foam cleanly. A slight forward push as each layer lands creates the folded, petal-like edges.

Pattern Three: The Rosetta

The rosetta, a leaf-like fern, is the hardest of the three because it combines movement and flow at once.

  1. Start at the back. After the base, drop low near the far third of the cup.
  2. Wiggle. Rock the pitcher side to side with a small, even wrist motion. White zigzags will appear and stack.
  3. Move backward. While wiggling, slowly draw the pitcher back toward the near edge of the cup. The zigzags compress into leaves.
  4. Finish. At the near edge, lift slightly and cut forward through the center to create the stem.

Keep the wiggle small, about 1 cm (under half an inch) side to side. Big movements create messy waves.

Practice and Next Steps

Pour ten hearts before attempting a tulip, and ten tulips before chasing a rosetta. Use whole milk while learning, then experiment with alternatives once your technique is consistent. Put your practice to use with our flat white and cappuccino recipes, which call for different foam depths. Above all, focus on milk quality: a perfect pattern in poorly textured milk is rare, while great microfoam almost pours a heart on its own.
