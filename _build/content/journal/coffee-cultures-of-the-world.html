Coffee is one of the few drinks found in nearly every country, yet how people brew, serve and share it varies enormously. In some places coffee is a ceremony of hospitality; in others it is a quick refuel at a counter, a reason to linger for hours, or a sweet treat on a hot street corner. These traditions reflect local history, climate, trade and taste. Here is a tour of some of the most distinctive coffee cultures, with tips on recreating a taste of each at home.

Italy: The Espresso Bar

In Italy, coffee usually means espresso, ordered simply as "un caffè." It is drunk quickly, often standing at the bar, sometimes several times a day. Many bars charge less at the counter than at a table, which reinforces the habit. The espresso is typically short and intense, and a glass of water is often served alongside.

Milk drinks follow loose customs. Cappuccino is widely considered a morning drink, with many Italians avoiding it after a large meal, while a macchiato, an espresso "marked" with a little foam, is acceptable any time. Practices vary by region and generation, so treat these as tendencies rather than rules. Try our cappuccino recipe for a classic version.

Ethiopia: The Coffee Ceremony

Ethiopia, coffee's birthplace, has one of the world's most meaningful coffee rituals. The ceremony, commonly hosted by a woman of the household, begins with washing green beans and roasting them in a flat pan over coals. The smoking beans are carried around so guests can enjoy the aroma. They are then ground by hand, traditionally with a mortar and pestle, and brewed in a clay pot with a narrow neck called a jebena.

Coffee is poured from height into small handleless cups, often with sugar and sometimes salt or herbs, and served with snacks such as popcorn or roasted barley. There are traditionally three rounds, each weaker than the last, and staying for all of them is considered polite. The whole ritual can take an hour or more and is as much about community and conversation as coffee. Learn more about the beans themselves in our Ethiopia origin guide.

Turkey and the Eastern Mediterranean

Turkish-style coffee is made by simmering extremely finely ground coffee with water, and optionally sugar, in a small long-handled pot, often called a cezve or ibrik. It is not filtered; the grounds settle in the cup, and the drinker stops before reaching them. A good cup has a thick layer of foam on top, and the sweetness level is chosen at the start, since sugar cannot be stirred in afterward.

Coffee here carries strong social meaning, from welcoming guests to traditions around engagement and fortune-telling from the grounds. Similar styles, with local variations in spice and roast, are found throughout the Balkans, the Levant and beyond, sometimes flavored with cardamom. Our Turkish coffee guide shows you how to make it.

Vietnam: Phin, Condensed Milk and Egg Coffee

Vietnam is one of the world's largest coffee producers, growing mostly robusta, and its café culture is lively and inventive. The classic brewer is the phin, a small metal drip filter that sits on top of a cup and slowly drips strong, dark coffee. The most famous drink pours it over sweetened condensed milk, either hot or over ice, producing an intense, sweet, chocolatey glass.

Hanoi is known for egg coffee, in which egg yolk is whipped with sugar and condensed milk into a custardy foam spooned over coffee. Coconut coffee and yogurt coffee are other popular variations. Street cafés with tiny plastic stools are a defining sight. Try our Vietnamese iced coffee recipe to taste the classic.

Japan: Kissaten and Precision Brewing

Japan's kissaten are traditional cafés that flourished in the 20th century. Many feel like time capsules, with wood paneling, soft lighting, classical or jazz music and a careful, unhurried pace. Coffee is often brewed one cup at a time by hand, using cloth-filter drip, paper pour-over or siphon brewers, and is commonly paired with thick toast, sandwiches or desserts.

That attention to detail extends to modern specialty cafés, which helped popularize precise pour-over and the technique of brewing hot coffee directly onto ice. Japan also has a huge market for canned coffee, sold hot or cold from vending machines, which shows the country's equal love of convenience.

Scandinavia: Light Roasts and Fika

The Nordic countries consistently rank among the world's highest coffee consumers per person. Filter coffee is the everyday staple, and Nordic roasters are known for very light roasts that highlight fruity, tea-like qualities.

In Sweden, fika describes a coffee break with something sweet, often a cinnamon or cardamom bun, shared with others. It is built into many workplaces and is valued as a social pause. Finland has a strong tradition of regular coffee breaks too, and in parts of Norway and Finland, boiled coffee remains a familiar rustic method.

Australia and New Zealand: Café Culture and the Flat White

Australia and New Zealand built a café culture around espresso and finely textured milk, shaped in part by Italian and Greek immigration after the Second World War. Independent cafés dominate, and quality expectations are high. The flat white, a small, strong milk drink with a thin layer of velvety microfoam, is closely associated with both countries, which have long debated where it was first made. Brunch is a serious affair, and baristas are respected professionals. Our flat white recipe recreates the style.

Colombia and Latin America

In Colombia, a small cup of black coffee, often called a tinto, is sold everywhere from street vendors to offices, frequently sweetened with panela, an unrefined cane sugar. Historically, much of the best coffee was exported, but a growing domestic specialty scene now showcases local beans. In other Latin American countries, café de olla in Mexico, brewed in a clay pot with cinnamon and piloncillo sugar, and the sweet, strong cafecito of Cuban tradition are beloved.

From Brazil's ever-present cafezinho to Spain's café con leche and Greece's frothy iced coffee, the list could go on. Each tradition shows that coffee is never only a beverage. It is a way of welcoming, pausing and belonging. For how these rituals came to be, read our history of coffee.
