Ask five people how much caffeine is in a cup of coffee and you will get five confident, different answers. They might all be right. Caffeine content depends on the species of bean, how much coffee goes in, how it is extracted and, most of all, how big the cup is. This guide gives you realistic typical ranges, explains what moves the numbers up and down, and shows how to estimate your own daily total without guesswork.

What Changes the Caffeine in Your Cup

Bean species

This is the single biggest variable you can control. Arabica beans typically contain about 1.2–1.5% caffeine by dry weight, while robusta commonly runs 2.2–2.7%, nearly double. Many espresso blends and most instant coffees include some robusta, which is one reason a small cup can hit harder than expected. Our arabica vs robusta guide explains the flavor trade-offs.

Dose and ratio

Caffeine is very soluble, so most of it ends up in the cup during a normal brew. That means the amount of ground coffee you use matters more than almost anything else. Brewing 15 g of coffee into 250 ml gives a lighter cup than 20 g into the same volume, even with identical beans. If you weigh your dose, you already have a good handle on your intake.

Brew time and temperature

Hotter water and longer contact extract caffeine more completely, but because caffeine dissolves early and readily, these effects are smaller than people assume. Cold brew compensates for cold water with very long steeps (12–24 hours) and high coffee-to-water ratios, which is why concentrates can be potent. See cold brew vs iced coffee for how the two methods compare.

Roast level

Roasting barely changes caffeine. The myth that dark roast is stronger, or that light roast is much stronger, mostly comes from measuring by scoop. Dark beans lose more mass and expand more, so a scoop of dark roast contains slightly less coffee by weight. Measure in grams and the difference nearly disappears.

Serving size

The most overlooked factor of all. A "cup" in a nutrition reference is 240 ml, but many mugs hold 350–475 ml (12–16 fl oz), and large takeaway sizes go well beyond that. Doubling the volume roughly doubles the caffeine.

How Much Caffeine Is Too Much?

The US Food and Drug Administration cites 400 mg per day as an amount that is not generally associated with negative effects for most healthy adults. In practical terms, that is roughly two to four typical cups of brewed coffee, depending on strength and size. You can read the agency's consumer guidance on its caffeine information page.

That figure is general guidance, not a target or a personal limit. People differ a great deal in how quickly they clear caffeine and how strongly they react to it. Commonly reported signs of having had more than suits you include jitteriness, a racing heartbeat, an upset stomach, anxiety and trouble sleeping.

Note: This is general information, not medical advice. If you are pregnant, trying to become pregnant, breastfeeding, take regular medication, or have a heart, sleep, anxiety or digestive condition, talk to a clinician about what caffeine intake is right for you.

Sensitivity, Timing and Sleep

Caffeine's effects are commonly described as peaking within about an hour, and its half-life in healthy adults is often cited as roughly three to six hours. In plain terms, half the caffeine from a 3 p.m. coffee may still be in circulation in the early evening. Genetics, age, pregnancy, some medications and smoking status can all shift that timeline noticeably.

A few practical habits help regardless of your tolerance:

  • Set a personal cutoff time. Many people find that stopping caffeine eight or more hours before bed makes a difference.
  • Spread it out. Two moderate cups several hours apart feel very different from one oversized cup drunk quickly.
  • Watch hidden sources. Tea, cola, energy drinks, chocolate and some pain relievers contain caffeine too.
  • Taper rather than quit cold. Cutting back gradually over a week or two tends to be gentler than stopping abruptly.

Estimating Your Daily Total

A simple method works well enough for everyday purposes:

  1. List every drink. Include coffee, tea, soft drinks and energy drinks from a typical day.
  2. Note the real size. Measure your usual mug in milliliters once; it is often larger than you think.
  3. Apply a typical value. Use the middle of the ranges above, or the high end if you brew strong or use robusta blends.
  4. Add it up and compare. If you land near or above 400 mg and feel fine, that is useful information; if you feel jittery at 200 mg, that is useful information too.

Lowering Caffeine Without Losing Flavor

Cutting back does not have to mean giving up the ritual. Try a smaller cup of better coffee instead of a large cup of average coffee. Blend half regular and half decaf beans, which lets you keep your brew method and grind unchanged. Choose 100% arabica over robusta-heavy blends. Or switch your afternoon cup to a quality decaf; modern decaffeination has improved a great deal, as our decaf explainer describes.

Pro tip: If you want a milky drink with less caffeine, order a single-shot latte rather than a double. You keep the texture and cut the caffeine roughly in half.

The Bottom Line

There is no single caffeine number for coffee, only typical ranges. Weigh your dose, know your cup size, prefer arabica if you want a gentler cup, and treat the FDA's 400 mg figure as broad guidance for healthy adults rather than a personal prescription.
