The V60-style cone is a deceptively simple tool: a 60-degree funnel with spiral ribs and a single large hole at the bottom. That big opening means the paper filter, not the brewer, controls how fast water drains, which puts you in charge of contact time through grind size and pouring technique. The payoff is a clean, aromatic, high-clarity cup that shows off what a good bean actually tastes like. The trade-off is that small mistakes show up clearly too. This guide gives you a dependable starting recipe and a method for adjusting it with confidence.

Ratio
1:16 (range 1:15–1:17)
Dose
15 g coffee
Water temp
93–96°C (199–205°F)
Grind
Medium-fine, like coarse table salt
Brew time
2:45–3:30 total
Yield
About 220 ml in the cup from 250 g water

What you need

A cone dripper (ceramic, glass, plastic or metal all work; plastic holds heat surprisingly well), a matching paper filter, a server or mug, and freshly roasted whole beans. Two tools matter more than any others: a burr grinder and a digital scale. A blade grinder produces a mix of boulders and dust that drains unevenly, and measuring by scoop can be off by 20 percent or more depending on roast and bean density. If you are choosing equipment, our grinder buying guide explains what consistency actually means in practice.

A gooseneck kettle is the third upgrade. It lets you pour a thin, steady stream at about 4–6 g per second and place it exactly where you want. Water quality matters as well: very hard water mutes acidity, and very soft or distilled water tastes flat and hollow.

The method

  1. Heat the water. Bring about 500 g of water to a boil, then let it settle for 30–45 seconds to land in the 93–96°C range. Use the hotter end for light roasts and the cooler end for dark roasts.
  2. Rinse the filter. Fold the paper along its seam, seat it in the cone, and pour hot water through to wet it fully and preheat everything. Discard that water.
  3. Grind and level. Grind 15 g of coffee medium-fine and add it to the filter. Give the cone a gentle shake so the bed is flat, then tare the scale.
  4. Bloom. Start a timer and pour 40–45 g of water, about three times the dose, in a spiral that wets every particle. Swirl the cone gently once. Wait until 0:45 while carbon dioxide escapes and the bed swells and cracks.
  5. First main pour. From 0:45, pour in slow concentric circles from the center outward and back, avoiding the paper walls, until the scale reads 150 g at about 1:15.
  6. Second main pour. At about 1:30, when the water level has dropped a little, continue pouring to 250 g by roughly 1:55.
  7. Swirl and drain. Give the dripper one gentle swirl to flatten the bed and knock grounds off the walls. Let it drain completely. A flat bed with no high ring of grounds signals an even extraction.
  8. Serve. Total time should be 2:45–3:30. Swirl the server to mix the stronger early coffee with the lighter late coffee, then pour.

Pro tip: Our brew timer can call out the bloom and pour checkpoints so you can keep your eyes on the kettle instead of the clock.

Why each step matters

Freshly roasted coffee is full of trapped carbon dioxide. If you pour all the water at once, that gas pushes water away from the grounds and channels form. The bloom gives the gas somewhere to go, so the main pours can saturate the bed evenly. Splitting the main pours keeps the slurry at a steady temperature and depth, and pouring away from the paper stops water from running down the sides and bypassing the coffee altogether.

The final swirl is about geometry. Grounds stranded high on the filter walls stop extracting early, while those at the bottom keep going. Leveling the bed puts every particle through roughly the same amount of water.

Dialing in your V60

Change one variable at a time, and change grind first. Grind size controls both how much flavor water can pull from each particle and how long the water takes to drain, so it has the biggest effect on the cup.

  • Use total time as your compass. If the brew finishes under 2:30, grind finer. If it runs past 3:45, grind coarser. Then judge by taste.
  • Adjust strength with ratio, not grind. If the cup tastes balanced but too thin, move toward 1:15. If it is balanced but heavy, move toward 1:17.
  • Match temperature to roast. Dense, light-roasted beans often need water near 96°C to extract fully. Darker roasts dissolve more easily and can turn harsh above 92°C. Our roast levels guide explains why.
  • Keep notes. Record dose, grind setting, water temperature and total time. After three or four brews you will know your grinder's sweet spot for each coffee.

To scale the recipe up or down, the brew ratio calculator will give you water amounts for any dose. For larger batches above about 30 g, coarsen the grind slightly because the deeper bed slows drainage.

Troubleshooting the cup

If it tastes...Try...
Sour, sharp, thin, saltyGrind finer, use hotter water (95–96°C), or extend the bloom to 45 seconds. These are signs of under-extraction.
Bitter, dry, astringentGrind coarser, drop to 92–93°C, or pour more gently to avoid stirring up fines.
Both sour and bitterUneven extraction. Check for channeling, level the bed, pour away from the walls, and consider a more consistent grinder.
Weak or watery but pleasantUse a stronger ratio such as 1:15 rather than changing grind.
Flat and mutedCheck bean freshness and water. Beans past about six weeks from roast and very hard water both dull aroma.
Papery or cardboard notesRinse the filter more thoroughly with hot water before brewing.

Choosing beans for pour-over

Paper filtration removes most oils and fine particles, so the V60 favors coffees with clear, distinct flavors: washed Ethiopian and Kenyan coffees with floral or berry acidity, or bright Central American lots. Light to medium roasts, rested 7–21 days after roasting, usually perform best. Very dark roasts can work, but they tend to taste one-dimensional through paper compared with a French press. If you want a similar style with a heavier filter and a longer, more forgiving brew, the Chemex is a natural next step.

Care and consistency

Rinse the cone after each use and wash it with mild soap weekly to remove coffee oils that turn rancid. Ceramic and glass drippers benefit from a longer preheat. Above all, keep your process consistent: same pour heights, same timings, same order. Consistency is what turns a single great cup into a great cup every morning.
