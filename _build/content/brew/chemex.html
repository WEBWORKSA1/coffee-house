The Chemex is an hourglass-shaped glass brewer with a wooden collar, and it is part laboratory flask, part design object. What sets it apart in the cup is the filter: a heavy, bonded paper that is substantially thicker than standard cone filters. It traps almost all oils and fine particles, producing coffee with exceptional clarity and a light, silky body. Compared with a V60 pour-over, the Chemex is slower and a little more forgiving of pouring technique, and it is excellent at brewing for two to four people. Its quirks, a slow filter and an air channel that can seal shut, are easy to manage once you understand them.

What you need

A Chemex of a suitable size (the 6-cup model handles 300–900 ml comfortably), Chemex-style bonded filters, a gooseneck kettle, a scale, a burr grinder and a timer. A gooseneck matters here: the Chemex holds a lot of water and the cone is wide, so you want controlled spirals rather than a heavy splash. If you are choosing a kettle, see our pour-over kettle guide.

The method

  1. Heat water. Bring about 1 liter of water to a boil and let it settle for around 30 seconds to reach 94–96°C. The thick filter and large glass body lose heat, so err on the hot side.
  2. Seat and rinse the filter. Open the filter into a cone with three layers on one side and one on the other. Place the thick side against the pouring spout. Rinse thoroughly with hot water to remove paper taste and preheat the glass, then pour the rinse water out through the spout while holding the filter in place.
  3. Add coffee. Grind 30 g of coffee medium-coarse, add it to the filter, and shake gently to level. Tare the scale.
  4. Bloom. Start the timer and pour 60–70 g of water evenly over the grounds. Wait 45 seconds while the bed degasses.
  5. First pour. At 0:45, pour in steady spirals to 200 g by about 1:30, keeping the stream in the center two-thirds of the bed.
  6. Second pour. At around 2:00, when the level has dropped by a third, pour to 350 g.
  7. Final pour. At around 2:45, pour to 500 g. Keep the water level below the top edge of the filter.
  8. Drain and serve. Let it drain fully, aiming for 4:00–4:45 total. Remove the filter, swirl the Chemex to mix, and pour.

Why the Chemex behaves differently

The bonded filter is the key. Its density slows water significantly, so a Chemex needs a coarser grind than most drippers to land in a sensible time. Slower flow also means a longer contact time overall, which helps extraction from those larger particles. The filter's thickness removes nearly all coffee oils, which strips away heaviness but also some of the rich, rounded texture a French press gives. That is why Chemex coffee often tastes lighter and more tea-like even at the same strength.

The spout on the side of the glass also serves as an air channel. If the wet filter collapses into it, air cannot escape as coffee fills the lower chamber, and the brew can stall. The triple-folded side of the filter is stiff enough to keep that channel open, which is why orientation matters.

Dialing in your Chemex

  • Use total time to guide grind. Under 3:45 and tasting thin: grind finer. Over 5:00 and tasting dull or bitter: grind coarser.
  • Adjust pour structure. More, smaller pours keep the temperature higher and increase agitation, which extracts more. Fewer, larger pours are gentler.
  • Watch for stalls. If drainage stops completely, lift the filter slightly from the spout side for a moment to release trapped air.
  • Scale sensibly. Bed depth changes with batch size. Larger batches need a slightly coarser grind; smaller ones a slightly finer grind. Use the brew ratio calculator to set water amounts.

Troubleshooting the cup

If it tastes...Try...
Thin, sour, underdevelopedGrind finer, keep water at 96°C, and add an extra pour to raise agitation.
Bitter, dry, mutedGrind coarser. A stalled brew over 5:30 nearly always over-extracts.
Flat and lukewarmPreheat the glass more thoroughly and pour in smaller, more frequent stages.
PaperyRinse the thick filter generously, using at least 200 g of hot water.
Too light in bodyUse a 1:15 ratio, or try the same beans in a cone dripper or French press for more texture.
Brew stalls halfwayCheck the filter's triple side faces the spout and that the grind is not too fine.

Brewing larger and smaller batches

The Chemex is at its best for two to four people, but the recipe flexes. For a single mug, use 20 g of coffee and 320 g of water, grind one or two steps finer, and use three pours instead of four; the shallow bed drains quickly and needs the extra resistance. For a large batch of 45 g to 720 g, grind a little coarser and extend the bloom to 60 seconds, because the deeper bed traps more gas and drains slowly. Expect a total time closer to 5:00–5:30 at that size. Whatever the batch, pre-plan your pour targets on paper or in the brew timer so you are not doing math with a full kettle in your hand. Keeping the water level steady and below the rim of the filter matters more as batches grow, since overflowing water can slip between paper and glass and bypass the coffee entirely.

Choosing beans

Because the Chemex removes so much oil and sediment, it suits coffees with expressive, layered flavors: washed African coffees with floral and citrus notes, delicate Central American lots, and light to medium roasts in general. Our page on Ethiopian coffee describes many of the profiles that shine in this brewer. Darker roasts work fine but can taste thin.

Care and cleaning

Hand-wash the glass with warm soapy water and a soft bottle brush; coffee oils build up as a brown film on the inside surface over time. Remove the wooden collar before washing if it is tied on with a leather cord. A soak in warm water with a little baking soda or a dedicated coffee cleaner lifts stubborn residue. Always let the glass cool before rinsing with cold water to avoid thermal shock.
