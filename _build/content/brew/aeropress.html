The AeroPress is a plastic brewing chamber with a plunger and a small filter cap. You steep coffee in water for a minute or two, then press it through a paper disk with gentle hand pressure. That short combination of immersion and pressure produces a clean, full-flavored cup in about two minutes, and the brewer survives travel, camping and office drawers. Its flexibility is its superpower: change grind, temperature and steep time and you can make anything from a delicate filter-style mug to a dense concentrate for milk drinks. That same flexibility can be confusing, so this guide anchors you with two well-tested starting points.

What you need

The brewer itself, paper filters, a sturdy mug that fits under the cap, a scale and a kettle. A burr grinder makes the biggest difference to consistency. If you travel, a compact hand grinder pairs perfectly with the AeroPress. Unlike a pour-over, you do not need a gooseneck kettle, since all the water goes in at once. A small stirring paddle comes in the box; any spoon works.

The standard method (everyday mug)

  1. Prepare the filter. Place a paper filter in the cap, rinse it with hot water, and twist the cap onto the chamber. Set the chamber on a sturdy mug.
  2. Add coffee. Grind 15 g medium-fine and pour it into the chamber. Shake level. Put the whole setup on a scale and tare.
  3. Pour and stir. Start a timer and add 230 g of water at 90–94°C. Stir briskly 3–4 times to wet every particle.
  4. Seal. Insert the plunger about 1 cm into the chamber. This creates a vacuum that stops most coffee from dripping through while it steeps.
  5. Steep. Wait until 1:30. Optionally lift the setup and give it a gentle swirl at 1:00 to keep grounds moving.
  6. Press. Press down slowly and steadily for 20–30 seconds. Stop when you hear a hiss; pressing through the dry puck pushes out bitter compounds.
  7. Serve. Total time is about 2:00. Remove the cap over the trash and push out the puck. Cleanup is done.

The inverted method (fuller immersion)

In the inverted method you flip the brewer upside down, with the plunger inserted a short way into the bottom of the chamber, so the open end faces up. Nothing drips through, so all the water steeps for the full time. Add 15 g of coffee and 220 g of water, stir, and steep for 1:45. Screw on the rinsed filter cap, then carefully flip the brewer onto your mug in one confident motion and press for about 30 seconds. The cup is slightly heavier and sweeter than the standard method. Always use a stable, heat-resistant mug and keep your hand over the cap while flipping, since a toppled brewer full of hot water can burn.

A concentrate for milk drinks

For a strong, short brew to top with milk, use 18 g of coffee ground finer than usual, 100 g of water at 85–88°C, a 1-minute steep and a firm 30-second press. You get roughly 80 ml of intense coffee at about a 1:6 ratio. It is not espresso, but it makes a respectable homemade flat white or iced drink. For the real thing, see our espresso at home guide.

Dialing in your AeroPress

  • Start with temperature. The AeroPress is unusually responsive to it. If a light roast tastes sour, raise toward 96°C. If a dark roast tastes harsh, drop to 85°C.
  • Adjust grind next. Finer grinds extract more and press harder. If pressing takes more than 40 seconds with real effort, grind coarser.
  • Use steep time for fine-tuning. Add or remove 30 seconds rather than making big jumps.
  • Bypass to scale. Brew a stronger concentrate and add hot water afterward to make a larger mug. This is called bypass, and it lets a small brewer serve a big cup. Our brew ratio calculator helps you work out the dilution.

Common mistakes to avoid

The most frequent error is grinding too fine in the hope of a stronger cup. Very fine coffee makes the plunger hard to push, stirs up bitterness and can blow fines past the paper. If you want more strength, add coffee or reduce water instead. The second is pressing through the puck after the hiss, which squeezes harsh, drying compounds into the last few milliliters. Third, many people use water straight off a rolling boil for every coffee. The AeroPress extracts efficiently, so dark and medium roasts usually taste sweeter at 85–90°C. Finally, measuring by the printed numbers on the chamber is imprecise because the grounds displace water; a scale gives you repeatable results and makes it possible to change one variable at a time.

Care and storage

Rinse the plunger seal and chamber after each use. Store the brewer with the plunger pushed all the way through or removed, rather than compressed inside the chamber; leaving the rubber seal squeezed for weeks flattens it and weakens the vacuum. Wash with mild soap occasionally to remove oils, and replace the seal if pressing starts to leak air. For more on keeping all your gear fresh, see our cleaning and descaling guide.

Choosing beans

The AeroPress handles almost any coffee well, and it is a good way to compare beans side by side because each brew is quick and uses little coffee. Paper filtration keeps the cup clean, so bright washed coffees taste vivid, while the pressure and immersion bring out sweetness in medium roasts. If you are unsure where to start, a medium roast from Central or South America is reliable. Once you know the brewer, try a light Ethiopian at 96°C and a long steep to see how far it can go.
