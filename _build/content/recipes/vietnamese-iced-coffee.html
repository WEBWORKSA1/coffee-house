Vietnamese iced coffee, known in Vietnamese as cà phê sữa đá (coffee, milk, ice), is one of the world's great coffee drinks. Strong, dark coffee drips slowly through a small metal filter onto a layer of sweetened condensed milk; stirred together and poured over ice, it becomes rich, sweet and intensely refreshing.

What it is and where it comes from

Coffee was introduced to Vietnam in the nineteenth century, and the country has since become one of the world's largest coffee producers, growing mostly robusta. Condensed milk is commonly said to have become popular because fresh milk was scarce and hard to keep in the tropical climate. The phin filter, cheap and durable, became the everyday brewer in homes and street stalls. Our coffee cultures of the world article looks at Vietnam's café traditions in more depth.

Flavor and texture

The coffee is bold and bitter, with dark chocolate, roasted nut and sometimes smoky notes, and the condensed milk adds caramel sweetness and a thick, creamy body. Ice tempers both into a cold, silky, strongly flavored drink. Robusta-heavy blends bring more bitterness and body; arabica-heavy blends are smoother and softer. Our arabica vs robusta page explains the difference.

Technique notes

The phin's press plate controls the flow. Rest it gently on the bed of grounds; pressing hard makes the drip painfully slow, while leaving it loose makes the coffee run through too fast and taste thin. Aim for steady drops, roughly one or two per second, and a total drip of 4–5 minutes. If it runs in under three minutes, grind finer or press the plate a little more firmly next time. Use plenty of ice: the hot, concentrated coffee melts a fair amount and that dilution is part of the recipe.

Variations

  • Hot version (cà phê sữa nóng): skip the ice and serve in a small cup, often set in a bowl of hot water to keep warm.
  • Black iced coffee (cà phê đá): omit the condensed milk and add sugar to taste.
  • Coconut coffee: blend the coffee with coconut milk, a little condensed milk and ice for a slushy texture.
  • Dairy-free: use sweetened condensed coconut milk.
  • No phin: brew dark-roast coffee in a moka pot instead.

Common mistakes

Using light-roasted specialty coffee often tastes sour against the condensed milk; a dark roast is traditional for good reason. Not stirring before adding ice leaves the condensed milk stuck at the bottom. Pressing the plate too hard can stall the drip completely.

Serving tips

Serve in a tall glass with a long spoon so drinkers can keep stirring as the ice melts. For a similar creamy, sweet drink built on espresso, try our Spanish latte.
