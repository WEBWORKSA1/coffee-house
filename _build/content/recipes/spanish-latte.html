A Spanish latte is a latte sweetened with condensed milk. That one change transforms the drink: it becomes richer, creamier and gently caramel-flavored, with a texture that feels almost like dessert while still tasting clearly of coffee.

What it is and where it comes from

Spain has a long tradition of pairing espresso with condensed milk, from small layered drinks to sweet versions of the cortado. The larger milk-drink version known as a Spanish latte became a café favorite in many countries, especially across the Middle East and Southeast Asia, in recent years. Despite the name, you are more likely to find it under that label outside Spain than in a Spanish café.

Flavor and texture

Condensed milk is milk that has been cooked down with sugar, so it brings both sweetness and the mellow, toasty flavors of slow-cooked dairy. It adds body as well, making the drink feel thicker than a standard latte. The espresso should still be the backbone, with chocolate and caramel notes that blend smoothly with the milk.

Technique notes

Dissolve the condensed milk in hot espresso first. Condensed milk is thick and sinks; if you add it to cold milk, it will stay at the bottom of the glass. Medium to medium-dark roasts with nutty and chocolate profiles pair best, while very bright, acidic coffees can clash with the sweetness. For hot versions, steam milk a little cooler than usual, around 60°C, since the drink is already rich. Our espresso at home guide will help you pull a sweet, balanced shot.

Variations

  • Iced Spanish latte: the most popular version; build over plenty of ice with cold milk.
  • Less sweet: use 15 ml of condensed milk and 15 ml of evaporated milk for richness without as much sugar.
  • Cinnamon: add a pinch of cinnamon to the espresso and condensed milk mixture.
  • Plant-based: use condensed coconut or oat milk with oat milk.
  • Layered café bombón style: use a small glass with equal parts condensed milk and espresso, unstirred, for a striking two-layer look.

Common mistakes

Overdoing the condensed milk makes the drink syrupy and masks the coffee. Not stirring it into the espresso leaves a thick layer at the bottom. Using a weak single shot also leaves the drink tasting mostly of sweet milk.

Serving tips

Serve iced in a clear glass to show the gradient of coffee and milk, or hot in a warmed ceramic cup. If you enjoy the sweet, creamy style, you will likely also enjoy Vietnamese iced coffee, which uses condensed milk with phin-brewed coffee.
