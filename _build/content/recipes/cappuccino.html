The cappuccino is a short, balanced espresso drink with roughly equal emphasis on coffee, milk and foam. Smaller than a latte, it tastes noticeably stronger, and the deep foam gives it a light, cloud-like texture that many coffee drinkers consider the perfect breakfast cup.

What it is and where it comes from

The cappuccino is strongly associated with Italy, where it became widespread as espresso machines capable of steaming milk spread through the twentieth century. Its name is often linked to the brown hooded robes of Capuchin friars, a reference to the drink's color, though the exact origin of the name is debated. In Italy it is traditionally a morning drink, rarely ordered after a meal.

Flavor and texture

With less milk than a latte, the espresso shines through clearly: chocolate, caramel and roasted notes with a soft sweetness from the milk. The foam is the defining feature. It should be thick enough to hold a spoon briefly, yet fine-textured and glossy rather than bubbly and dry. Sipping through the foam into the warm coffee beneath is part of the experience.

Technique notes

A cappuccino needs more air than a latte, so stretch the milk for longer at the start: keep the wand tip right at the surface until the milk has grown by about a third. Stretch only while the jug still feels cool, then submerge the tip and let the whirlpool polish the foam into a uniform microfoam. Swirl the jug constantly before pouring, because thick foam separates quickly from the liquid. A well-pulled shot is the foundation, so see our espresso at home guide if your coffee tastes sour or bitter.

Variations

  • Dry cappuccino: more foam and less liquid milk for a lighter, more intense drink.
  • Wet cappuccino: less foam and more steamed milk, closer to a small latte.
  • Plant-based: barista-style oat milk foams well; soy foams readily but can curdle with very acidic espresso.
  • Spiced: dust with cinnamon or nutmeg instead of cocoa.

Common mistakes

The most common fault is adding air too late, once the milk is already hot, which creates big bubbles that pop quickly. Another is scooping foam on with a spoon, which produces a dry, separated drink instead of a blended one. Overheating past 70°C kills sweetness and makes the foam collapse faster.

Serving tips

Use a wide, warmed cup of 150–180 ml so the foam has room to form a dome. Serve immediately: cappuccino foam is at its best in the first few minutes.
