Coffee is the product your customers come for, yet many new café owners choose a roaster on a single tasting and a price sheet. A better approach treats the decision like choosing a business partner: estimate your volume, shortlist several roasters, ask the same questions of each, understand how their pricing is structured, and sample on your own equipment before signing. This guide walks through each step.

Estimate Your Volume First

Roasters price and prioritize based on volume, so know your numbers before you call. An illustrative example:

  • 220 transactions per day, of which about 80% include a coffee drink: roughly 176 coffee drinks.
  • Average coffee per drink of about 19 g, mixing 18 g espresso doses and slightly larger brewed portions.
  • 176 × 19 g = about 3.3 kg per day, plus a 10% allowance for dialing in and waste = about 3.7 kg per day.
  • Open seven days: about 25.7 kg (57 lb) per week, or roughly 1,340 kg per year.

That annual figure is why small price differences matter. At this volume, every USD 1.00 per kilogram is worth about USD 1,340 a year. Seen per drink, an 18 g shot costs about USD 0.36 at USD 20 per kg and about USD 0.47 at USD 26 per kg. Plug the per-drink difference into your costings using our coffee menu pricing guide to see how much a more expensive coffee changes your beverage cost.

What a Good Roaster Partner Provides

  • Consistency. The house espresso should taste the same in March as in October, which means careful blending and green coffee buying as harvests change.
  • Freshness and reliable delivery. A clear schedule and roast-to-delivery window.
  • Training. Many wholesale roasters offer barista training, recipe guidance and dialing-in help at opening.
  • Technical support. Some provide equipment programs, service referrals or water advice.
  • Transparency. Clear information on origins, producers and processing, useful for your menu and staff knowledge.
  • Responsiveness. Someone who answers when the grinder misbehaves at 7 a.m. on a Saturday.

Common Wholesale Pricing Structures

Pricing varies by roaster and market, so these are general patterns rather than benchmarks:

  • Tiered per-kilogram pricing. The price per kg falls as your weekly or monthly volume rises.
  • Price lists by coffee. Blends are often priced lower than single origins; rare or competition-grade lots cost more.
  • Fixed-term pricing. A price held for a set period, giving you predictability but possibly with a volume commitment.
  • Market-linked pricing. Prices adjust periodically as green coffee costs change.
  • Equipment-linked agreements. A roaster provides or finances equipment in exchange for an exclusive supply commitment, sometimes at a slightly higher per-kg price. Read terms carefully: understand who owns the equipment, who repairs it and what happens if you leave early.
  • Delivery and small-order fees. Some roasters add charges below a certain order size.

Compare total cost over a year, not just the headline per-kg price. A slightly higher price that includes training, service support and reliable delivery may be better value.

How to Run a Sampling Process

  1. Shortlist three to five roasters whose style suits your concept. Read about roast levels so you can describe what you want: bright and fruity, balanced, or rich and chocolatey.
  2. Request samples of the house espresso and a brewed coffee, and say how you plan to serve them, including the share of milk drinks.
  3. Cup the brewed samples side by side, ideally blind, using the same ratio, grind and water. Our guide to how to taste coffee explains a simple structured tasting method.
  4. Test espresso on equipment like yours. Taste each as straight espresso and in a milk drink at your intended dose and yield. Many cafés sell far more milk drinks than straight shots, so test what you will actually sell.
  5. Involve your team. Your lead barista's palate and opinion matter, and buy-in helps with consistency.
  6. Score each coffee on flavor, consistency across a few days, ease of dialing in, price and support. A simple weighted score keeps the decision objective.
  7. Run a short trial, such as a few weeks of real service, before committing to a long agreement.

Green vs Roasted: Should You Roast In-House?

Roasting your own coffee can offer control, a strong story and potentially lower per-kilogram costs at volume. It also creates a second business inside your café. Consider the trade-offs:

FactorBuy roasted wholesaleRoast in-house from green
Upfront costNone beyond inventoryRoasting machine, installation, ventilation and possibly emissions control
SkillRoaster supplies expertiseRequires roasting knowledge and consistent profiling
Weight lossNot your concernGreen coffee loses roughly 12–20% of its weight in roasting, depending on roast degree
Space and permitsMinimalDedicated space, possible zoning, fire and air-quality approvals
Green sourcingHandled by the roasterImporters, sampling, contracts and storage of green coffee
LaborNoneRoasting, cooling, packing, quality control
UpsideFocus on serviceWholesale and retail revenue, full control of flavor

For most new cafés, buying roasted coffee from a trusted partner is the lower-risk start. Roasting often makes more sense later, once volume, retail demand and your own skills justify the investment. Permit and emissions rules for roasting differ significantly by city; check with local authorities before planning a roastery.

Managing Supply Once You Open

  • Order on a fixed rhythm, matched to your weekly usage plus a small buffer.
  • Rotate stock first in, first out, and store sealed bags cool, dry and away from light. See our guide to storing coffee beans.
  • Log usage weekly against sales to catch waste or portioning drift early.
  • Review the relationship twice a year: quality, service, price and how your needs have changed.
