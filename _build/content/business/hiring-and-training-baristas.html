Labor is usually a café's largest controllable cost, and the team is what customers remember. Hire too few people and the line stalls; hire too many and the margin disappears. Hire the wrong people and neither matters. This guide works through staffing in a practical order: model the hours you need, calculate what they cost, recruit and select carefully, then train to a written standard.

Build the Schedule Before You Hire

Start with an hourly sales forecast. Most coffee-led cafés have a sharp morning peak, a smaller lunch bump and a quieter afternoon. Staff to that shape, not to a flat headcount. A useful starting point is to estimate how many drinks one barista can comfortably produce per hour on your equipment, then divide your peak-hour forecast by that figure.

Here is an illustrative schedule for a small café open 7:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m. on weekdays and 8:00 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. on weekends:

ShiftHoursPaid hours per day
Weekday opener 16:30–14:308.0
Weekday opener 26:45–13:006.25
Weekday peak support7:00–10:003.0
Weekday mid and close11:00–17:306.5
Weekend (two baristas)7:30–16:3018.0

Weekdays total 23.75 hours × 5 days = 118.75 hours, plus 36 weekend hours, for about 155 paid hours per week.

Calculate Labor Cost

Using the schedule above, assume an illustrative average wage of USD 18 per hour and payroll costs (employer taxes, benefits, paid leave) adding about 20%, for USD 21.60 per hour. That gives:

  • 154.75 hours × USD 21.60 = about USD 3,343 per week
  • × 52 ÷ 12 = about USD 14,485 per month
  • Against illustrative sales of USD 49,500 per month, labor is about 29% of sales

Many independent cafés land somewhere around 25–35% of sales on labor, so this example sits in a workable range. Note that it excludes any salary for the owner or manager. Wages, payroll taxes, minimum-wage rules, overtime, breaks and tip regulations vary widely by country and city; confirm them with local employment authorities and an accountant. Write a Clear Job Description

A good posting attracts the right applicants and filters out the wrong ones. Include:

  • Role and schedule, including early starts and weekend requirements.
  • Pay range and any tips policy, stated honestly.
  • What the day involves: espresso and milk drinks, cash handling, cleaning, restocking, customer service during rushes.
  • Whether experience is required or whether you will train.
  • What makes your café a good place to work: training, consistent hours, staff drinks, growth into lead roles.

Recruit through local job boards, hospitality networks, café community groups, nearby colleges and personal referrals. Your roaster partner may know experienced baristas looking for a move.

Interview and Paid Working Trial

Hire for attitude and train for skill. Technique can be taught in weeks; warmth, reliability and calm under pressure are much harder to teach. Useful interview questions explore how the candidate handles a long queue, a mistaken order, an unhappy customer, or a coworker who is struggling. Ask about availability and commute, since early-morning reliability matters enormously.

Many cafés follow the interview with a short working trial behind the bar. Whether trials must be paid, and how they must be structured, depends on local employment law; paying candidates for their time is good practice and often required. During the trial, watch for:

  • How they greet and talk to customers.
  • Whether they clean as they go without being asked.
  • How they take instruction and respond to feedback.
  • Their awareness of teammates in a tight space.

A Two-Week Pre-Opening Training Plan

For a new café, plan at least one to two weeks of paid training before opening day. A sample structure:

1–2Coffee basics, café standards, food safety, equipment orientationTeam understands the concept, recipes and hygiene rules
3–4Grinding, dosing, distribution, tamping, extraction and dialing inConsistent shots within recipe targets
5–6Milk steaming and texturing for each drink, basic latte artCorrect texture and temperature every time

Use a written checklist so every barista is trained and signed off on the same standards.

After Opening: Ongoing Training and Quality Checks

Training does not end on opening day. The first six to eight weeks of real service reveal habits that drills never surface, so keep a structured ramp-up in place:

  1. Daily calibration. The opening barista dials in espresso against the written recipe and logs dose, yield, time and taste before service. A second person tastes the first shot.
  2. Weekly check-ins. Spend ten minutes with each new hire reviewing one strength and one skill to work on, using the checklist above.
  3. Shadow shifts for new hires. Pair every new barista with an experienced one for their first several rush periods before scheduling them in a lead position.
  4. Monthly cupping. Taste your current coffees together, ideally with your roaster, so the team can describe them confidently to customers.
  5. Spot checks. Weigh a random milk pour or shot each week. Small drifts in portioning affect both consistency and your cost of goods, as explained in our menu pricing guide.

Write every recipe and routine down in one shared manual. When a question comes up, answer it, then update the manual so the next person gets the same answer.

Keeping Good Baristas

Turnover is expensive: every departure means recruiting, training hours, lower speed and a dip in consistency. Practical retention levers include:

  • Predictable schedules, published well in advance.
  • Fair, transparent pay and a clear tips policy, compliant with local rules.
  • A growth path from barista to trainer, shift lead and manager.
  • Ongoing coffee education, such as cuppings with your roaster.
  • Regular feedback, both recognition and coaching, in short check-ins.
  • A well-maintained workplace where equipment works and stock is ready.
