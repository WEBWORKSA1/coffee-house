A coffee bean is the seed of a fruit. Before it can be roasted, the seed must be separated from the skin, pulp, sticky mucilage and parchment around it, then dried from about 60% moisture down to roughly 10–12%. Producers have developed several ways to do this, each shaped by climate, water availability, tradition and market demand. The method chosen has a profound effect on taste, and it is one of the most useful pieces of information on a coffee label.

Anatomy of a coffee cherry

From the outside in, a ripe cherry has a thin skin, a layer of sweet fruit pulp, a slippery coating of mucilage, a papery shell called parchment, a delicate silver skin, and finally the seed, usually two facing halves. Processing methods differ mainly in how much of the fruit stays in contact with the seed during fermentation and drying, and for how long. More fruit contact generally means more sweetness, body and fruit flavor, along with more risk of defects if things go wrong.

Washed (wet) process

In the washed process, cherries are depulped soon after picking, removing skin and most of the pulp. The seeds, still coated in mucilage, are fermented in tanks with or without water, typically for 12–72 hours depending on temperature, until the mucilage breaks down. They are then washed in clean water and dried in parchment on patios, raised beds or mechanical dryers.

Washed coffees tend to taste clean, bright and transparent, letting origin and variety speak clearly. It is the standard method in Colombia, Kenya and much of Central America. Its downside is water use and the wastewater it produces, which must be managed responsibly.

Natural (dry) process

The oldest method: whole cherries are spread out to dry in the sun, on patios or raised beds, and turned regularly for roughly 3–6 weeks until they shrivel to a dark husk. The dried fruit is then hulled off. Natural coffees are typically fruity, sweet and full-bodied, with notes of berries, tropical fruit or wine. The method needs little water, which suits dry regions such as parts of Ethiopia and Brazil. Poorly managed drying can cause mold or over-fermentation, giving musty or boozy off-flavors, so careful turning and sorting are essential.

Honey and pulped natural

A middle path. The skin and pulp are removed, but some or all of the mucilage is left on the seed during drying. In Central America this is called honey processing, and producers often grade it by how much mucilage remains and how dark the parchment turns during drying. Brazil's pulped natural is a close relative.

  • White honey: most mucilage removed; closest to washed.
  • Yellow honey: a moderate amount left; dried faster, often in sun.
  • Red honey: more mucilage, slower drying; sweeter and fruitier.
  • Black honey: the most mucilage and slowest, shaded drying; closest to natural.

Honey coffees usually combine the sweetness and body of a natural with some of the clarity of a washed coffee. Definitions vary between producers, so treat the color names as a guide rather than a standard.

Wet-hulled (giling basah)

Common in Indonesia, especially Sumatra. Farmers depulp and briefly ferment coffee, then partially dry it before selling. The parchment is removed while the bean is still at high moisture, and drying finishes on the bare green bean. This speeds up the process in a humid climate but exposes beans to the environment, which contributes to the low acidity, heavy body and earthy, herbal flavors typical of Sumatran coffee.

Anaerobic and experimental fermentation

Experimental methods have spread rapidly through specialty coffee. In anaerobic fermentation, cherries or depulped seeds are sealed in tanks with little or no oxygen, sometimes with a one-way valve to release carbon dioxide, and fermented under controlled time and temperature. Carbonic maceration, borrowed from winemaking, flushes tanks with carbon dioxide. Other methods involve extended fermentation, added yeast cultures or co-fermentation with fruit. Results can be intense: tropical fruit, cinnamon, rum or yogurt-like notes. Some drinkers love these coffees; others find them overpowering. Labels that disclose added ingredients help buyers make an informed choice.

How processing affects brewing

Processing changes how coffee behaves in your brewer, not just how it tastes. Naturals and anaerobic lots can be prone to fines and extract quickly, so a slightly coarser grind and a gentler pour often help. Washed light roasts are dense and may need a finer grind or hotter water. Try comparing a washed and a natural from the same farm side by side; it is the clearest lesson in coffee tasting you can get. Use our flavor wheel to put words to the differences.

Pro tip: If a natural tastes boozy or like overripe fruit in a bad way, try brewing it at a stronger ratio and slightly cooler temperature. If it still tastes fermented, the issue is the coffee, not your technique.
