Grind size is the most powerful variable most home brewers can control. Finer particles expose more surface area, so water extracts flavor faster; coarser particles extract more slowly. Every brew method is designed around a particular contact time and filter, and the right grind keeps extraction in a sweet, balanced zone. This chart gives you a starting point for each method; your taste buds and your grinder do the rest.

Why grind size matters

Brewing is a race between extraction and time. Coffee contains acids, sugars and bitter compounds that dissolve at different rates: acids come out first, sugars and body next, and harsher bitter compounds last. A grind that is too coarse for the method lets water pass before the sweetness has been extracted, so the cup tastes sour, thin and salty. A grind that is too fine slows the flow and over-extracts, giving bitterness, astringency and a hollow finish. The right size balances the two.

Grind size also affects flow. In pour-over and espresso, finer particles pack more tightly and slow the water. That is why grind adjustments often change brew time, which is a useful diagnostic.

How to dial in your grind

  1. Start from the chart. Set your grinder near the recommended size and keep your dose, ratio and water temperature fixed.
  2. Brew and taste. Note sourness (under-extraction) or bitterness and dryness (over-extraction).
  3. Adjust in small steps. Go finer if sour or weak, coarser if bitter or harsh. Change one setting at a time.
  4. Watch the time. Use a brew timer; if your brew runs much faster or slower than the typical range, grind is the likely culprit.
  5. Record what works. Note the setting for each coffee, since different beans and roasts need different settings.

Uniformity: the hidden variable

Average size is only half the story. Grinders produce a distribution of particles, including very fine "fines" and oversized "boulders." Fines over-extract and add bitterness and can clog filters; boulders under-extract and add sourness. Burr grinders produce far more consistent results than blade grinders, which chop beans randomly and create a wide spread of sizes. Pro tip: Pour a little ground coffee on a white sheet of paper and spread it with a finger. An even texture with few dusty fines or large chunks is a sign of a good grind.

How roast and bean affect grind

  • Light roasts are dense and harder to extract; you will often need a slightly finer setting.
  • Dark roasts are brittle and soluble; grind slightly coarser to avoid bitterness.
  • Fresh beans release carbon dioxide that can disrupt extraction; very fresh espresso coffee may need adjustments as it rests.
  • Bigger doses in pour-over increase brew time; grind a touch coarser when brewing larger batches.

Pre-ground coffee and grinding fresh

Ground coffee loses aroma rapidly, with noticeable changes within minutes and significant staling within days. Grinding just before brewing is one of the biggest upgrades you can make. If you buy pre-ground coffee, choose a grind made for your method, store it airtight, and use it within a week or two. See our guide to storing coffee beans for more.

Troubleshooting by taste

If your coffee is sour, thin or salty, grind finer. If it is bitter, harsh or drying, grind coarser. If it is both sour and bitter at once, the grind is likely uneven, or the pour or tamp is causing channeling, so focus on grind consistency and technique before chasing a new setting.
