Most brewing advice focuses on beans, grind and ratio, but water is the largest ingredient in every cup. A filter coffee is roughly 98–99% water, and espresso is about 90%. Water is also the solvent that does the extracting, and its mineral content changes how well it does that job. Get water right, and the same beans taste sweeter, cleaner and more vivid.

The four numbers that matter

  • Hardness: mostly calcium and magnesium. These minerals help bind and extract flavor compounds. Magnesium is often associated with brighter, fruitier extraction; calcium with body and rounder flavor.
  • Alkalinity (buffer): mostly bicarbonate. It neutralizes acids. A little preserves balance; too much flattens acidity and sweetness.
  • Total dissolved solids (TDS): the overall concentration of everything dissolved in the water, measured in mg/L or ppm.
  • pH: acidity or basicity. Neutral, around 7, is the usual target, though alkalinity matters more for taste than pH alone.

Water also needs to be free of chlorine and odors, which carry straight into the cup. Note that "hard" and "alkaline" are related but not the same: two waters can share similar hardness yet have very different alkalinity, and alkalinity is often the bigger influence on taste.

How to find out what is in your water

Many water utilities publish annual water quality reports that list hardness and alkalinity. Inexpensive test strips can give rough readings for hardness, alkalinity and chlorine, and a handheld TDS meter measures total dissolved solids. Note that TDS alone does not tell you whether minerals are the helpful kind, so combine it with hardness and alkalinity readings.

Practical options for better water

  1. Carbon filtration. Pitcher and tap filters remove chlorine and odors. Some also reduce hardness. A good first step for most homes.
  2. Bottled spring water. Choose one with moderate mineral content and low sodium; check the label for TDS or mineral breakdown. Avoid heavily mineralized waters.
  3. Mineral packets or concentrates. Added to distilled or reverse osmosis water to create a consistent, coffee-specific recipe. Popular with enthusiasts.
  4. Reverse osmosis with remineralization. Common in cafés and some homes; strips nearly everything, then adds back a controlled mineral profile.

Pro tip: Compare your tap water with a moderate spring water using the same beans and recipe. If the difference is obvious, water is worth addressing.

Water and your equipment

Hard water with high alkalinity deposits limescale when heated. Scale coats heating elements, clogs narrow pipes and valves, and is one of the most common reasons espresso machines fail. Soft but highly corrosive water, such as pure reverse osmosis or distilled water used in machines without minerals, can also damage metal components over time. Balanced water protects both taste and equipment. Read our cleaning and descaling guide and, if you are shopping, our espresso machine buying guide covers water management features.

Warning: Always follow your machine manufacturer's water recommendations. Some machines are not designed for very soft or demineralized water, and using it can affect sensors or void warranties.

Water temperature

Chemistry matters, but so does heat. Most brewing works best between about 90 and 96°C (194–205°F). Lighter roasts generally like the upper end, darker roasts the lower end. At high altitude, water boils below 100°C (212°F), so a kettle taken straight off the boil is already cooler than at sea level; account for this if you live in the mountains. Always start with freshly drawn cold water rather than water that has sat in the kettle and been reboiled, which can taste flat.
