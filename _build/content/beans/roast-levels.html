Green coffee tastes grassy and beany. Roasting transforms it, building hundreds of aromatic compounds through browning reactions and caramelization. How far a roaster takes the beans changes almost everything about the cup, from acidity and sweetness to body and bitterness. Roast names are not standardized, so learning what actually happens at each stage is more useful than memorizing labels.

What happens during roasting

A typical roast takes roughly 8–15 minutes. As beans heat, they first dry and turn yellow, then brown as Maillard reactions and caramelization begin. Around 196–205°C (385–401°F) bean temperature, water vapor and gases fracture the beans with an audible crack, known as first crack. Light roasts finish shortly after. If roasting continues, the bean structure breaks down further and a second, quieter crack begins around 224–230°C (435–446°F); oils migrate to the surface and roast flavors dominate. These temperatures are approximate and vary by roaster and measurement method.

Beans lose moisture and mass during roasting, commonly 12–20% of their weight depending on how dark they go, and expand in size, becoming more porous and brittle.

Light roasts

Light roasts preserve the character of the coffee's origin, variety and processing. They tend to show the most acidity and aromatic complexity: think jasmine and lemon in a washed Ethiopian or blackcurrant in a Kenyan. Because the beans are denser and less soluble, they are harder to extract. Brew them with hotter water, around 94–96°C (201–205°F), and a slightly finer grind than you might expect.

Medium roasts

Medium roasts balance origin character with roast-derived sweetness. Acidity softens, caramel and nutty flavors build, and body increases. Most people find medium roasts the easiest to enjoy and to brew, and they work across nearly every method. Water at 92–94°C (198–201°F) is a reliable starting point.

Medium-dark and dark roasts

As roasting continues past the start of second crack, roast flavors take over. Medium-dark coffees deliver chocolate, spice and syrupy body with low acidity. True dark roasts become smoky and bitter, and the oily surface means they stale faster. Dark roasts extract easily, so use cooler water, around 88–91°C (190–196°F), and a slightly coarser grind to avoid harshness. Dark roasts also make robust moka pot coffee and stand up to milk and sugar.

Note: Darker does not mean stronger. Strength is about how much coffee is dissolved in your cup, which depends on your ratio. Use the brew ratio calculator to adjust strength independently of roast.

Roast level and caffeine

A persistent myth holds that dark roast is more caffeinated. In reality, caffeine is relatively heat-stable, and differences by weight are small. Because dark roast beans lose more mass and become less dense, a scoop of dark roast holds slightly less coffee than a scoop of light. If you weigh your coffee, as you should, the difference is minor.

Matching roast to brew method

  • Pour-over and filter: light to medium, where clarity and aroma matter most.
  • Espresso: medium to medium-dark is the traditional choice; light espresso needs careful dialing in.
  • French press and cold brew: medium to dark, for body and chocolatey depth.
  • Milk drinks: medium-dark roasts cut through milk best.

Whatever you choose, roast freshness matters as much as level. See our guide to storing coffee beans to keep your coffee at its best.

Roast date and resting

Freshly roasted coffee releases carbon dioxide for days. Brewing it too soon can cause uneven extraction and a sharp, gassy taste. As a rough guide, filter coffee often tastes best from about 3–7 days after roasting, and espresso from about 7–14 days, with lighter roasts sometimes needing longer to settle. Darker roasts degas faster and also fade faster, so drink them sooner. Always buy from roasters who print a roast date rather than only a best-before date.

How to choose a roast

If you like fruit, tea and wine, start light. If you want a classic, smooth cup, choose medium. If you love bold, bittersweet coffee with milk, go medium-dark. Because roast labels vary between roasters, use tasting notes as your guide: "berry, citrus, floral" signals a light roast, "chocolate, caramel, nut" a medium, and "smoky, roasty, dark chocolate" a darker roast.
