How the brew ratio works

A brew ratio is simply the weight of coffee compared with the weight of water. At 1:16, every gram of ground coffee meets sixteen grams of water. Ratio is the main lever for strength — how concentrated the finished cup feels — while grind size, time and temperature control extraction, which decides whether the cup tastes sour, sweet or bitter.

That distinction matters when you troubleshoot. If a pour-over tastes watery but also sweet and clean, tighten the ratio from 1:17 to 1:15. If it tastes sharp and hollow, the ratio is probably fine and you need a finer grind. Our brew guides each include a troubleshooting table that maps taste to the right adjustment.

Three tips for consistent results

  1. Weigh, don't scoop. Bean density varies with roast level, so a "tablespoon" can be anywhere from 5 to 8 grams. A 0.1 g scale removes the guesswork.
  2. Change one variable at a time. Set your ratio, then dial in grind. Changing both at once makes it impossible to learn what helped.
  3. Write it down. Keep a note of dose, water, grind setting and time for every bag. Use the brew timer to repeat a recipe exactly.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best coffee to water ratio?

For most filter methods, 1:15 to 1:17 (one gram of coffee for every 15–17 grams of water) is the sweet spot. Espresso is far more concentrated at around 1:2, and cold brew concentrate sits near 1:8.

How many grams of coffee for one cup?

For a 250 ml mug of filter coffee, use about 15–16 g of coffee at a 1:16 ratio. Use the calculator above to match your exact cup size.

Should I measure water in grams or milliliters?

For water, 1 ml weighs almost exactly 1 g at brewing temperatures, so either works. Weighing on a scale is more accurate than reading a jug.

Why does my coffee taste weak even at the right ratio?

Ratio controls strength, but extraction controls flavor. If coffee tastes thin and sour, grind finer or brew longer; if it tastes bitter and dry, grind coarser.
