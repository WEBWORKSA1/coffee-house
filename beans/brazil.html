Brazilian Coffee: Regions, Flavor and How to Brew It

Brazil grows more coffee than any other country. Its nutty, chocolatey, low-acid beans are the backbone of espresso blends, and its best farms produce standout single origins.

6 min readBy the Coffee.House editorial teamUpdated October 2026How we write

Brazil is the giant of the coffee world, producing far more than any other country and supplying a large share of global Arabica. Its scale can hide its quality: alongside vast commodity estates, Brazil has dedicated specialty farms producing sweet, complex coffees. Understanding Brazil helps you understand espresso, because so many blends are built on its comforting, nutty, chocolatey profile.

Typical altitude
800–1,300 m (2,600–4,300 ft), higher in parts of Minas Gerais
Harvest season
Roughly May to September
Main processing
Natural, pulped natural
Common varieties
Mundo Novo, Catuaí, Yellow Bourbon, Catucaí, Acaiá
Flavor profile
Peanut, milk chocolate, caramel, low acidity, full body

Why Brazil tastes the way it does

Brazilian coffee grows farther from the equator than most, so the climate is cooler at lower altitudes. Farms at 900–1,200 m on rolling plateaus produce beans that are less dense than high-grown Central American or East African coffee, which translates to softer acidity and a rounder, heavier body. The dry harvest season allows coffee to be dried in the cherry on vast patios, adding sweetness and body. The combination gives Brazil its signature: smooth, nutty and chocolatey, with a low bite.

Major growing regions

Sul de Minas

The southern part of Minas Gerais is Brazil's traditional heartland, with hilly terrain, smaller farms and elevations that can reach 1,400 m or more. Coffees are sweet, balanced and sometimes fruity.

Cerrado Mineiro

A high, flat savanna plateau with clearly defined wet and dry seasons. Its flat land allows mechanized harvesting, and the region has a formal designation of origin. Expect consistent, chocolatey, nutty coffees.

Mogiana

Spanning the border of São Paulo and Minas Gerais, Mogiana is known for sweet, full-bodied coffees with caramel and cocoa notes.

Chapada Diamantina and Bahia

Further north, high-altitude areas in Bahia have earned a reputation for cleaner, fruitier lots that challenge the low-acid stereotype.

Espírito Santo

Known for both mountain Arabica and lowland conilon (Robusta), making it an important region for both species. Learn more in our guide to Arabica vs Robusta.

Processing in Brazil

Brazil is synonymous with dry processing. In a natural, whole cherries are spread on patios or raised beds and raked frequently until dry, often over two to four weeks. Brazil also popularized the pulped natural, where the skin is removed mechanically but the sticky mucilage stays on during drying; it is a close relative of the Central American honey process. Washed coffee exists but is less common. Each method shifts the profile, as explained in our guide to processing methods.

Harvesting at scale

On flatter farms, Brazilian coffee is often harvested by machines that shake branches, or by strip-picking, where all cherries are pulled from a branch at once. These methods are efficient but collect ripe and unripe fruit together, so quality depends heavily on sorting afterward. Specialty farms may selectively hand-pick or use careful density and color sorting to separate the best cherries.

A brief history

Coffee is generally believed to have arrived in Brazil in the early 18th century. Through the 19th century it became the country's dominant export, fueling major economic growth and, it must be acknowledged, relying heavily on enslaved labor until abolition in 1888. By the early 20th century Brazil was producing the majority of the world's coffee, and it has remained the largest producer ever since, with an industry that now spans small family farms to highly mechanized estates.

How to brew Brazilian coffee

Brazil is made for espresso: try 18 g in, 36 g out in 27–32 seconds at 92–93°C (198–199°F) for a sweet, chocolatey shot that pairs beautifully with milk. A moka pot brings out rich cocoa and toasted nut. For a heavy, comforting filter, a French press at 1:15 works well, and Brazilian beans make smooth, low-acid cold brew.

Pro tip: Brazilian naturals can taste flat at very dark roasts. A medium roast keeps the caramel sweetness and nutty character without tipping into bitterness.

Buying tips

  • Choose Brazil for milk drinks, blends and anyone who prefers low acidity.
  • Look for region names and the process: natural for body, pulped natural for balance.
  • Higher-scoring lots from Sul de Minas or Bahia can surprise you with fruit and sweetness.
  • Brazil is often roasted for espresso; if you brew filter, ask for or look for a lighter "omni" or filter roast.
  • Because the harvest runs mid-year, new-crop Brazilian coffee usually reaches Northern Hemisphere roasters from late in the year onward.
  • Naturals vary widely in cleanliness. A well-sorted lot tastes sweet and nutty; a poorly sorted one can taste woody or fermented.

Frequently asked questions

What does Brazilian coffee taste like?

Brazilian coffee typically tastes of peanut, almond, milk chocolate and caramel, with full body and low acidity. Top lots can add fruit and brown sugar sweetness.

Why is Brazilian coffee used in espresso blends?

Its low acidity, heavy body and chocolatey sweetness create a smooth, crema-rich base that pairs well with milk and balances brighter coffees.

What is pulped natural coffee?

It is a method where the skin is removed but the sticky fruit layer is left on the seed during drying, giving a cup between washed and natural in sweetness and body.

Does Brazil grow Robusta?

Yes. Brazil grows a large volume of Robusta-type coffee, locally called conilon, mostly in lower, warmer states such as Espírito Santo and Rondônia.
