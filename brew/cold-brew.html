How to Make Cold Brew Coffee at Home (1:8 Concentrate)

Cold brew trades heat for time. Steep coarse coffee for most of a day and you get a low-acid, chocolate-sweet concentrate that keeps for days and dilutes to taste.

7 min readBy the Coffee.House editorial teamUpdated October 2026How we write

Cold brew is made by steeping coarsely ground coffee in cold or room-temperature water for many hours, then filtering. Without heat, water dissolves acids and aromatic compounds more slowly and less completely, so the result tastes rounder, sweeter and less sharp than hot coffee, with deep chocolate and nutty notes. It is also convenient: one batch of concentrate covers several days of iced drinks. The method is simple, but a few details separate silky cold brew from the muddy, woody versions that give it a bad name. The recipe below makes about 750 ml of concentrate, enough for six to eight drinks.

Ratio
1:8 concentrate (dilute about 1:1)
Dose
100 g coffee to 800 g water
Water temp
Cold or room temperature, 4–22°C (39–72°F)
Grind
Coarse, like cracked pepper
Brew time
14–18 hours
Yield
About 600–650 ml concentrate after absorption

What you need

A large jar or pitcher (at least 1 liter), a burr grinder, a scale, filtered water, and a way to strain: a fine mesh sieve plus a paper filter, a nut milk bag, or a French press, which makes the first strain easy. Dedicated cold brew systems with fabric or paper filters work well too, but are not required. Because cold water extracts slowly, water quality has an outsized influence. Our water for coffee page explains why softer, lightly mineralized water tastes cleaner.

The method

  1. Grind coarse. Grind 100 g of coffee coarse, a little coarser than French press. Too fine a grind is the most common reason cold brew turns muddy and bitter.
  2. Combine. Add the grounds to your jar and pour in 800 g of cold filtered water.
  3. Stir thoroughly. Stir for 15–20 seconds so no dry clumps float on top. Dry pockets do not extract and waste coffee.
  4. Steep. Cover and leave for 14–16 hours at room temperature or 18–20 hours in the fridge. A fridge steep tastes a touch cleaner and is the safer choice in a warm kitchen.
  5. First strain. Pour through a fine mesh sieve or press the plunger of a French press to remove the bulk of the grounds. Do not squeeze the grounds; that pushes bitter fines into the liquid.
  6. Second strain. Pour the liquid again through a paper filter. This is slow, sometimes 10–20 minutes, but it removes the silt that makes cold brew taste chalky and helps it keep longer.
  7. Store. Transfer to a clean, sealed bottle and refrigerate immediately.
  8. Serve. Mix concentrate 1:1 with cold water or milk over ice. Adjust to taste, anywhere from 1:0.5 for a strong drink to 1:2 for a light one.

Why cold brew tastes different

Hot water dissolves bright organic acids and volatile aromatics rapidly. Cold water extracts sugars and heavier flavor compounds more readily than those bright acids, which is why cold brew comes across as mellow and low-acid even though its actual pH is only modestly different from hot coffee. It also misses much of the aroma that makes hot coffee smell so vibrant. That is a feature for people who find hot coffee sharp, and a limitation for delicate floral coffees whose best qualities are mostly aromatic. If you want bright iced coffee, brew hot directly over ice instead; our journal piece on cold brew vs iced coffee compares the two.

Concentrate vs ready-to-drink

A concentrate is the most flexible approach because you choose the strength at serving time and it takes up less fridge space. Ready-to-drink cold brew, made at 1:12 to 1:15 and steeped the same way, needs no dilution and gives a more consistent glass every time, which some households prefer. The trade-off is volume: a liter of ready-to-drink only covers four or five servings. Try both and keep whichever fits your routine.

Choosing beans

Medium to medium-dark roasts with chocolate, nut and caramel notes are the classic choice, and coffees from Brazil, Colombia and Central America do this beautifully. Very dark roasts can taste smoky and flat. Light, floral coffees can be surprising and fruity, but you will lose much of their perfume. Beans do not need to be as fresh as for pour-over, but avoid stale coffee, which tastes papery after a long steep.

Dialing in your cold brew

  • Change time before grind. If the concentrate tastes thin or tea-like, extend steeping by 2–4 hours. If it tastes woody or bitter, shorten it.
  • Use grind to fix texture. If filtering is painfully slow and the cup is silty, grind coarser.
  • Use ratio for strength. A 1:5 ratio makes a very strong concentrate for milk drinks; 1:10 is closer to ready to drink.
  • Think about caffeine. Concentrate is strong. Diluting 1:1 brings it closer to a regular mug, but undiluted servings can contain far more caffeine than you expect. Our caffeine calculator gives a rough estimate.

Troubleshooting the cup

If it tastes...Try...
Weak, watery, tea-likeSteep 2–4 hours longer, use a stronger ratio, or dilute less.
Woody, papery, flatShorten the steep and check the beans are not stale.
Bitter and harshGrind coarser and do not squeeze the grounds while straining.
Chalky or siltyAdd the paper-filter second strain and let the concentrate settle before pouring.
Sour or fermentedSteep in the fridge rather than a warm room, and use clean equipment.
Dull after a few daysMake smaller batches and keep the bottle tightly sealed and cold.

Storage and food safety

Cold brew is a low-temperature, long-contact preparation, so basic kitchen hygiene matters. Start with a clean jar and utensils, use filtered or potable cold water, and if your kitchen is warmer than about 22°C (72°F), steep in the refrigerator rather than on the counter. Once filtered, keep the concentrate sealed in the fridge at or below 4°C (40°F). Most home batches taste their best within about a week; after that aroma fades and a stale, flat note creeps in. Never leave diluted cold brew with milk sitting out at room temperature for more than a couple of hours. If the concentrate smells sour, fermented or off, discard it. Making smaller batches more often, for example 50 g of coffee to 400 g of water, is the simplest way to always drink it fresh.

Serving ideas

Try concentrate over ice with an equal amount of whole milk, or with tonic water and an orange twist for something bright and fizzy. Freeze some of the concentrate in ice cube trays so your drink does not water down as it melts. For a cold brew-based milk drink with more structure, use it in place of espresso in an iced latte, using about 60 ml of concentrate per glass.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best ratio for cold brew?

A 1:8 ratio by weight makes a concentrate that you dilute about 1:1 with water or milk. For ready-to-drink cold brew, use 1:12 to 1:15 and skip dilution.

How long should cold brew steep?

At room temperature, 12–16 hours. In the fridge, 16–24 hours. Beyond that, flavors tend to turn woody and flat rather than stronger.

How long does cold brew last in the fridge?

Filtered concentrate kept sealed and cold generally tastes best within about a week. Flavor fades gradually, so smell and taste before serving and discard it if it seems off.

Is cold brew the same as iced coffee?

No. Iced coffee is brewed hot and then chilled, often over ice, which keeps brightness and aroma. Cold brew never sees heat, so it is smoother and lower in perceived acidity.
