How to Make Turkish Coffee in a Cezve (Ibrik)

Turkish coffee is brewed unfiltered in a small pot and served with its grounds settled in the cup. Slow heat, a powder-fine grind and good timing give it a dense, velvety foam.

7 min readBy the Coffee.House editorial teamUpdated October 2026How we write

Turkish coffee is one of the oldest brewing styles still in everyday use, and it is closely associated with Turkey, the eastern Mediterranean, the Balkans and the Middle East, with many regional variations in name and method. Very finely ground coffee is simmered with water, and often sugar, in a small long-handled pot called a cezve (also known as an ibrik in some regions). It is never filtered. Instead, the coffee is poured gently into a small cup and given a minute to settle. The result is intense, full-bodied and aromatic, with a thick foam on top that is considered the mark of a well-made cup. In Turkey the tradition is recognized for its cultural significance, and preparing it well is a point of pride for hosts. Our article on coffee cultures of the world explores more of that context.

Ratio
About 1:10
Dose
7 g coffee per cup
Water temp
Start cold; finish just below boiling, around 95°C (203°F)
Grind
Powder-fine, like flour
Brew time
3–4 minutes on low heat
Yield
About 60–65 ml per demitasse cup

What you need

A cezve sized for the number of cups you are making (they range from one to six servings; a pot that is filled roughly halfway works best), small demitasse cups of about 70–90 ml, a teaspoon, and coffee ground to powder. Copper and brass pots are traditional and heat responsively; stainless steel and ceramic work too. If your grinder cannot go fine enough, use pre-ground coffee sold specifically for this method and keep it sealed. A gas flame or small electric burner on low gives the best control.

Choosing your sweetness

Because sugar is stirred in before heating, you decide sweetness at the start. Traditional levels are roughly:

  • Plain (sade): no sugar.
  • A little sugar (az şekerli): about half a teaspoon per cup.
  • Medium (orta): about one teaspoon per cup.
  • Sweet (şekerli): about two teaspoons per cup.

Sugar also helps foam form and lasts, which is one reason medium sweetness is so popular.

The method

  1. Measure the water. Use the serving cup to measure one cup of cold, fresh water per person, about 70 ml each, into the cezve.
  2. Add coffee and sugar. Add 7 g of coffee, about one heaped teaspoon, per cup, plus sugar if using. Stir gently until the coffee is fully mixed in and no dry powder floats on top.
  3. Heat slowly. Place the cezve on low heat. Do not stir again. The slow rise in temperature, typically 3–4 minutes, gives time for foam to develop.
  4. Watch for foam. A ring of fine foam forms around the edge and then spreads across the surface. The surface darkens and starts to puff upward.
  5. Divide the foam. When the foam begins to rise toward the rim, lift the pot off the heat. Spoon a little foam into each cup.
  6. Return briefly. Put the cezve back on the heat for a few seconds until it rises again, then lift it off before it boils.
  7. Pour slowly. Pour gently down the side of each cup so the foam floats intact. Fill each cup evenly.
  8. Rest and serve. Let the cups sit for about a minute so the grounds settle. Serve with a glass of water and something sweet alongside.

Pro tip: Lower heat gives better foam. If your coffee rises in under two minutes, turn the burner down; a rushed brew tends to taste thinner and produce large, short-lived bubbles.

Dialing in Turkish coffee

  • Grind is everything. If foam is thin and the cup tastes weak, the grind is probably too coarse. True powder-fine coffee is essential.
  • Adjust strength by dose. Use 8–9 g per cup for a bolder brew or 6 g for a lighter one.
  • Control heat. Aim for that slow 3–4 minute rise. Too fast and foam is coarse; too slow and the coffee can taste flat.
  • Fill the pot properly. A cezve filled only a quarter of the way heats unevenly and loses foam. Choose a pot that fits your batch.
  • Pick the right beans. Medium roasts are traditional and balance sweetness with body. Our roast levels guide explains the trade-offs.

Troubleshooting the cup

If it tastes...Try...
Weak, watery, no foamUse a finer grind and slightly more coffee, and heat more slowly.
Bitter, burntRemove it from the heat before it boils. A rolling boil scorches the coffee.
Gritty throughoutLet the cups rest longer before drinking and pour more gently.
Flat, staleUse fresher coffee. Powder-fine coffee loses aroma fast, so buy small amounts and store it airtight.
Foam collapses quicklyLower the heat, add a little sugar, and avoid stirring once heating begins.
Too sweet or not sweet enoughAdjust sugar at the start next time; it cannot be fixed afterward.

Variations and serving

Cardamom is a popular addition in many Middle Eastern traditions: add a pinch of ground cardamom or one crushed pod per cup with the coffee. Some households add a small amount of mastic or a drop of rose water. Serving traditions vary, but a glass of water to cleanse the palate and a small piece of something sweet are common companions. Turkish coffee is strong in flavor, yet each cup is small, so its caffeine per serving is often in a similar range to a single espresso; our caffeine guide compares typical amounts across methods. For another intensely flavored small coffee brewed on the stove, see our moka pot guide.

Brewing without a cezve

If you do not own a cezve, a very small saucepan with a pouring lip can work, though its wide base makes foam spread thin. Choose the narrowest pan you have, keep the heat low, and brew a double batch so the liquid is deep enough. Electric Turkish coffee makers are also common in homes where the drink is made daily; they heat slowly and switch off before boiling. Whatever vessel you use, the essentials do not change: powder-fine coffee, cold water to start, sugar stirred in at the beginning, no stirring once heating starts, and lifting the pot off before it boils.

Care and cleaning

Rinse the cezve right after use and wipe it dry to prevent water spots. Lined copper pots should be washed by hand without abrasive scrubbers, which can wear through the tin or steel lining. If the lining begins to show copper underneath, have it relined or replace the pot.

Frequently asked questions

What grind is used for Turkish coffee?

The finest possible grind, like flour or powdered cocoa, much finer than espresso. Most home burr grinders cannot reach it, so many people buy pre-ground Turkish coffee or use a dedicated hand grinder.

Should Turkish coffee boil?

No. Remove it from the heat as the foam rises, just before it boils. A rolling boil destroys the foam and makes the coffee taste harsh.

When do you add sugar to Turkish coffee?

Sugar goes in at the beginning, stirred into the cold water with the coffee. It cannot be added afterward without disturbing the grounds and foam.

Do you drink the grounds in Turkish coffee?

No. Let the cup rest for a minute so the grounds settle, then sip slowly and stop when you reach the thick sediment at the bottom.
