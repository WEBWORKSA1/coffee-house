How to Make French Press Coffee: Ratio, Grind & Time

The French press is the most forgiving full-immersion brewer you can own. Get the grind and the waiting right and it delivers a heavy, rounded cup with almost no grit.

7 min readBy the Coffee.House editorial teamUpdated October 2026How we write

A French press, or plunger pot, is full immersion brewing at its simplest: coffee and water sit together for a few minutes, then a metal mesh separates them. Because the mesh lets oils and some very fine particles through, the cup has more body and a rounder, heavier texture than paper-filtered coffee. It is also one of the most forgiving methods there is. Water temperature, pouring technique and timing all matter less than they do with a cone, which makes it ideal for brewing for several people or for anyone who wants great coffee without fuss. The secret to a truly clean press, though, is patience after the steep.

Ratio
1:15 (range 1:12–1:17)
Dose
30 g coffee for 450 g water
Water temp
93–96°C (199–205°F), just off the boil
Grind
Coarse, like sea salt
Brew time
4 min steep + 4–6 min settle
Yield
About 400 ml, two mugs

What you need

A French press of any size, a burr grinder, a scale, a kettle, and two spoons. Glass presses show you what is happening; double-walled steel presses hold temperature better over the long settle. A burr grinder matters more here than almost anywhere else, because the mesh filter cannot stop the dust that blade grinders make. If your grinder produces a lot of fines, that dust will end up as sludge at the bottom of the cup. See the grind size chart for a visual reference of what coarse should look like.

The method

  1. Preheat. Fill the empty press with hot water, swirl, and discard. A cold glass carafe can drop the brewing temperature by several degrees.
  2. Grind and dose. Grind 30 g of coffee coarse and add it to the press. Place the press on a scale and tare.
  3. Add all the water. Start a timer and pour 450 g of water just off the boil, aiming to wet all the grounds quickly. You do not need a gooseneck kettle; a fast, even pour is fine.
  4. Steep. Leave it alone for 4 minutes. A crust of grounds and foam forms on top. Do not plunge or stir.
  5. Break the crust. At 4:00, stir the top gently with a spoon. Most grounds will sink immediately.
  6. Skim. Use two spoons to scoop off the remaining foam and floating particles. This step removes much of the bitterness and grit.
  7. Wait. Place the lid on with the plunger raised and let the pot sit for another 4–6 minutes. Fine particles sink to the bottom while the coffee cools toward a pleasant drinking temperature.
  8. Press gently and pour. Lower the plunger only to the surface of the liquid; it is acting as a strainer, not a piston. Pour slowly and leave the last centimeter in the pot. Decant everything else into cups or a carafe.

Note: Extended settling does not make the coffee more bitter. Once the grounds have sunk below the liquid and stopped moving, extraction slows dramatically. The bitterness people blame on long steeps usually comes from pressing hard and stirring up fines.

Why this approach works

In an immersion brew, extraction happens fastest in the first couple of minutes and then levels off as the water becomes saturated with dissolved coffee. That is why the press is so forgiving: a minute longer or shorter barely changes the cup. The real enemy is turbulence. Plunging hard forces water through the compacted bed and kicks up the finest particles, which pass through the mesh and continue to extract in your mug. Skimming and settling solve both problems by letting gravity do the filtering before the mesh ever moves.

Dialing in your French press

  • Start with strength. If the cup is pleasant but thin, use a stronger ratio like 1:13. If it is heavy and tiring, go to 1:16 or 1:17.
  • Use grind for balance. Sour, hollow cups mean grind finer. Harsh, drying cups mean grind coarser. Change by one or two clicks at a time.
  • Use steep time as a fine adjustment. Extend to 5 minutes for a fuller extraction, or cut to 3 for darker roasts.
  • Scale freely. The method works for a 350 ml single press or a 1-liter pot. Plug your vessel size into the brew ratio calculator to get the dose.

Medium and darker roasts shine in a press because their sweetness and body suit the heavy texture. Brazilian and Sumatran coffees are classic choices; our Brazil origin page covers why those nutty, chocolate profiles work so well with immersion.

Troubleshooting the cup

If it tastes...Try...
Sour, thin, lemonyGrind finer, check the water was just off the boil, and steep the full 4 minutes before breaking the crust.
Bitter, harsh, ashyGrind coarser and press much more gently. Do not push the plunger to the bottom with force.
Muddy, silty, grittySkim the crust thoroughly, extend the settle, and leave the last of the pot behind. Check your grinder for excess fines.
Weak but cleanIncrease the dose to a 1:13 or 1:14 ratio.
Stale or rancidClean the mesh assembly thoroughly; old oils trapped in the screen go rancid quickly.
LukewarmPreheat the press and cups, or use an insulated press for the long settle.

Making cold brew and more in a press

The press doubles as a cold brew maker: combine coffee and cold water at about 1:8, steep in the fridge for 16–18 hours, then press and dilute. Our cold brew guide covers the full process. You can also use the plunger to froth warm milk by pumping it rapidly for 20–30 seconds, which gives a passable foam for a homemade latte if you do not own a steam wand.

Common mistakes to avoid

Pouring water that has cooled for several minutes is a frequent cause of flat, sour press coffee; use water just off the boil. Using pre-ground coffee intended for drip machines is another, since that grind is too fine for a mesh filter and produces a muddy cup. Leaving the finished coffee sitting on the grounds is the third, and it is the one that most often turns a good brew bitter. Decant as soon as you have pressed.

Cleaning and care

Empty grounds into compost or the trash rather than the sink, where they can clog drains. Unscrew the plunger assembly every week or two and wash each layer of mesh, spring plate and cross plate in warm soapy water. Coffee oils trapped between the layers turn rancid and add a stale note that no recipe can fix. Replace the mesh if it becomes bent or torn, since gaps at the edge let grounds slip past and undo all your careful settling.

Frequently asked questions

How long should French press coffee steep?

Four minutes is a reliable baseline before breaking the crust. Letting the grounds settle for another 4–6 minutes before pressing gives a cleaner cup without making it more bitter.

What grind size is best for a French press?

Coarse, roughly like sea salt or breadcrumbs. Too fine and the cup turns muddy and bitter; too coarse and it tastes thin and sour.

Why is my French press coffee gritty?

Fine particles pass through the metal mesh. Use a burr grinder, skim the foam after steeping, wait for the grounds to settle, press gently, and leave the last centimeter of coffee in the pot.

Can I leave coffee in the French press after brewing?

It is best to decant it right away. Coffee left on the grounds keeps extracting and turns bitter and stale within 10–15 minutes.
