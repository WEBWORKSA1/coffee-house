Coffee Shop Equipment Checklist by Station

Buying café equipment piece by piece leads to gaps and duplicates. Plan it by station instead, sized for your busiest hour, and use these checklists to make sure nothing is missing.

8 min readBy the Coffee.House editorial teamUpdated October 2026How we write

The fastest way to blow an equipment budget is to shop without a list. The second fastest is to buy for your average hour instead of your peak. This checklist is organized by station, the way a café actually works, and each table notes what to consider when sizing or specifying each item. Quantities assume a small coffee-led café of 10–30 seats serving perhaps 150–300 drinks a day; scale them up or down for your format.

Before You Buy: Size for Peak Demand

Estimate your busiest 60 minutes. If your plan calls for 250 transactions a day and 30% fall between 7:30 and 8:30 a.m., you need to serve 75 orders in that hour, and more drinks than that, since many orders include two. That number tells you how many group heads, grinders, milk pitchers and baristas you need. Equipment that is perfectly adequate at 40 drinks an hour becomes a bottleneck at 90.

Also confirm utilities before ordering anything. Commercial espresso machines often need dedicated high-amperage circuits, and many require a water line and drain within a set distance. Your electrician and plumber should review the spec sheets during the design stage, not on installation day.

Refrigeration and Ice Checklist

ItemTypical quantitySizing and spec notes
Under-counter fridge at the espresso bar1–2Holds milk within arm's reach of the steam wand
Reach-in or walk-in fridge1Back-of-house stock of dairy, alternative milks and food
Freezer0–1Needed for frozen pastry or ice cream
Ice machine1Size for peak summer iced-drink sales plus a buffer
Refrigerated display case0–1For grab-and-go food and bottled drinks
Fridge and freezer thermometers1 per unitLog temperatures as your food-safety plan requires

Work out refrigeration from your milk usage. As a rough planning figure, a 350 ml (12 oz) latte uses around 250–300 ml of milk once you allow for pitcher waste, so 150 milk drinks a day can consume roughly 40–45 liters (about 10–12 US gallons). With two or three deliveries a week, plus alternative milks and food, back-of-house cold storage fills quickly. Ice is similar: a 470 ml (16 oz) iced drink can take 150–200 g of ice, and summer demand can easily double your winter estimate. Choose an ice machine rated comfortably above your hottest-day forecast, because running out of ice at 2 p.m. in July is a lost afternoon of sales.

Smallwares Checklist

  • Ceramic cups in each menu size, plus saucers if you serve for-here drinks on them.
  • Glassware for iced drinks and water.
  • Disposable cups, lids, sleeves, straws and carriers, all matched in size.
  • Syrup pumps, sauce bottles and shakers.
  • Measuring jugs, scoops and labeled storage containers.
  • Sanitizer buckets, cloths, color-coded towels.
  • Sheet pans, tongs and serving utensils for food.
  • Dish racks, plus a commercial dishwasher or a compliant multi-sink setup.
  • Food-safe labels and date dots.
  • First aid kit and fire extinguisher as required locally.

Buying Strategy and Final Checks

  1. Draw the workflow first. Order, pay, espresso, milk, hand-off and pastry should flow in one direction without baristas crossing paths.
  2. Collect spec sheets. Power, water, drain and clearance requirements for every major item go to your contractor before build-out.
  3. Get two or three quotes for major equipment, and compare warranty length, local service coverage and installation.
  4. Decide buy, lease or used item by item. Furniture and stainless steel are low-risk used buys; espresso machines need professional inspection.
  5. Ask your roaster whether they offer equipment programs or service referrals as part of a supply agreement. See our wholesale sourcing guide.
  6. Check local requirements. Health, fire and electrical codes differ by country and city and may dictate sink counts, refrigeration specs and ventilation. Confirm with local authorities before ordering.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most important piece of coffee shop equipment?

The espresso machine gets the attention, but many experienced operators argue the grinder matters just as much, because grind consistency drives shot quality. Water treatment is the quiet third essential, since it protects both flavor and the machine.

How many group heads does a coffee shop need?

A two-group machine suits many small cafés. A three-group machine is often considered when peak demand regularly exceeds what two baristas can serve on two groups, or when you plan to run two espresso workflows at once.

Should I buy or lease café equipment?

Buying costs more upfront but nothing monthly; leasing preserves cash but adds a fixed monthly payment and total cost is usually higher. Compare the full cost over the lease term and check maintenance responsibilities before deciding.

Is used espresso equipment worth buying?

It can be, especially from a reputable dealer who has refurbished and tested it. Have a qualified technician inspect the boiler, pumps and electronics, and confirm that parts and service are available locally.
