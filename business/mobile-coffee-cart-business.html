Mobile Coffee Cart Business: Setup, Costs and Earnings

A coffee cart can test a concept, build a following and earn real money for a fraction of a café's capital. It also has its own constraints. Here is how to plan one that works.

8 min readBy the Coffee.House editorial teamUpdated October 2026How we write

A mobile coffee business trades seats and square meters for flexibility. You go where the customers are: farmers markets, office parks, festivals, weddings and corporate events. Startup capital is far lower than a fixed café, and you can test pricing, menu and demand before committing to a lease. The trade-offs are limited capacity, weather exposure, power and water logistics, and a calendar you have to fill yourself. This guide covers the decisions in order.

Power, Water and Workflow

Power

Power is the defining technical challenge. Commercial espresso machines are heavy electrical loads, especially while heating up, and grinders and refrigeration add to the draw. Check the rated wattage and amperage for each item and size your generator or battery system to the combined load with headroom. Options include:

  • Venue power ("shore power"): simplest, but confirm the available circuit and plug type in advance.
  • Inverter generator: flexible, but noise and fumes are restricted at many venues.
  • Battery and inverter system: quiet and clean, but sized carefully for a full shift and recharged between events.
  • Gas-heated or lever machines: some mobile operators use them to reduce electrical demand.

Water

You need a fresh-water tank, a waste-water tank (usually somewhat larger than the fresh tank), a pump, and filtration suitable for espresso. A busy shift uses water for espresso, hot water, rinsing pitchers and handwashing. Many health authorities require a dedicated handwashing sink. Hard water scales boilers quickly, and a mobile unit is hard to service mid-season, so filtration is not optional.

Workflow

Design the counter so the customer orders and pays at one end and collects at the other. With limited space, a short menu is essential. A typical mobile menu might be espresso, americano, flat white or cappuccino, latte, mocha, one or two iced drinks and a non-coffee option. Each extra item adds bottles, syrups and storage you may not have room for. See the equipment checklist for station-level detail, scaled down for your format.

Permits and Rules

Mobile vending rules vary considerably by country, region and city. Common requirements include a business registration, a mobile food vendor permit or license, a health inspection of the unit, food-handler certification, a commissary or base-of-operations agreement, fire safety checks for generators or gas, vehicle registration and insurance, liability insurance, and event-specific or street-trading permits. Some cities limit where and when you can trade on public streets. Contact your local health and licensing authorities before buying equipment, as their requirements may dictate your cart design.

Revenue Models and Pricing

  • Pay-per-drink at public events and markets. Revenue depends on traffic and weather; pitch fees may be flat or a share of sales.
  • Hosted bar for private events. The client pays a flat fee or per-guest rate for a set service window; guests drink free. Revenue is guaranteed.
  • Guaranteed minimum. Guests pay per drink, and the client tops up to a minimum if sales fall short.
  • Regular pitches. A fixed weekday spot at an office park, school or hospital builds repeat customers.

Price your drinks using real cost of goods; our coffee menu pricing guide walks through per-drink costing.

A Day in the Life: Operations Checklist

  1. Day before. Confirm the booking, venue access time, power arrangement and parking. Check weather and pack a wind and rain plan.
  2. Load-out. Fill fresh water, charge batteries or fuel the generator, and load milk and ice last so they stay cold. Use a printed packing list every time.
  3. Arrival and setup. Allow 45–90 minutes. The espresso machine needs time to reach temperature, and grinders need dialing in on site.
  4. Service. One person takes orders and handles milk; the other pulls shots, or both rotate. Track drink counts for your records.
  5. Close-down. Backflush the machine, empty waste water at an approved point, and leave the pitch clean.
  6. Back at base. Clean and sanitize, restock, log temperatures and sales, and note what ran short.

Setup and pack-down time is unpaid by customers, so include it in every quote. Many new operators underprice events because they count only service hours.

Filling the Calendar

  • Build a booking page with packages, service windows, guest-count ranges and what the client must provide (power, space, access).
  • Approach event planners and venues that host weddings and corporate functions; a reliable vendor list placement can bring steady referrals.
  • Pitch to offices for recurring staff appreciation or meeting service.
  • Use markets for marketing. Hand out cards or display a QR code to your booking page.
  • Collect photos and reviews from real events with permission, and post them consistently.

When the calendar is consistently full and you know your best neighborhoods, you have the evidence base for a fixed café. Our roadmap to opening a coffee shop picks up from there. To talk through whether a cart, kiosk or fixed site fits your goals, book a free launch consultation.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to start a coffee cart business?

As a rough estimate, many cart and kiosk setups fall somewhere in the range of USD 24,000 to 126,000 including equipment and a modest cash reserve, depending on whether you use a simple cart, a trailer or a fully fitted van. Get local quotes, as costs vary by market.

Can you run an espresso machine on a generator or batteries?

Yes, but commercial espresso machines draw a lot of power, so the generator or battery system must be sized to the machine's rated load and run time. Many operators use a quiet inverter generator or a large battery bank, and some events provide shore power.

Do I need a commissary kitchen for a coffee cart?

Many jurisdictions require mobile food vendors to operate from an approved base for cleaning, water filling and storage, but rules vary widely. Check with your local health authority before buying a cart.

Are private events more profitable than markets?

Often, because private bookings are usually priced as a flat fee or per guest, which guarantees revenue. Public markets and street pitches depend on weather and foot traffic, but they build visibility that leads to bookings.
