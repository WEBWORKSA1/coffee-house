Coffee Menu Pricing: Cost Per Drink and Target Margins

Good café pricing starts with knowing exactly what each drink costs you, down to the lid. Then it accounts for what customers will happily pay. Here is the math, with a worked example.

8 min readBy the Coffee.House editorial teamUpdated October 2026How we write

Many new café owners set prices by looking at the café down the street and subtracting a quarter. That tells you what the market tolerates, but nothing about whether you make money. The sound approach has two passes: first calculate the cost of goods sold (COGS) for every drink, then set prices that hit your target margin while fitting local expectations and customer psychology. This guide covers both, with a worked example you can copy.

What Goes Into Cost of Goods per Drink

COGS for a beverage is everything consumed in making and serving that one drink. Labor and rent are not included here; they are covered by the margin you leave above COGS.

  • Coffee: grams per drink × your cost per gram. An 18 g double shot at USD 22 per kg costs about USD 0.40.
  • Milk: milliliters used, including what stays in the pitcher, × cost per milliliter.
  • Flavorings: syrups, sauces and powders, measured by pump or gram.
  • Packaging: cup, lid, sleeve, straw. For for-here drinks, a small allowance for washing and breakage.
  • Waste allowance: dialing in grinders each morning, remakes, spilled milk and discarded batch coffee. Many cafés add 5–10% of the ingredient cost; track your real waste and adjust.

Use real prices from your supplier invoices. A roaster quote per kilogram is the starting point; our wholesale coffee sourcing guide explains how wholesale pricing typically works.

Walking through the latte

  1. Coffee. 18 g × USD 0.022 per gram = USD 0.396, rounded to 0.40.
  2. Milk. About 280 ml steamed for a 350 ml cup (allowing for foam and pitcher residue) × USD 0.0011 per ml = USD 0.31.
  3. Packaging. Cup, lid and sleeve, estimated at USD 0.20.
  4. Waste. 10% of coffee and milk (0.71) = USD 0.07.
  5. Total. 0.40 + 0.31 + 0.20 + 0.07 = USD 0.97, or 18.6% of a USD 5.25 price.

Setting a Target Beverage Cost

Many independent cafés aim for a blended beverage cost of roughly 20–30% of sales, once packaging, waste, staff drinks and comps are included. The blended figure matters more than any single drink: a low-cost espresso and drip program can carry a few higher-cost specialty drinks.

To price from a target, divide cost by the target percentage:

Price = COGS ÷ target beverage cost %

Example: the oat latte costs USD 1.56. At a 25% target, 1.56 ÷ 0.25 = USD 6.24, so a USD 6.25 price works. At the USD 6.00 shown above, it runs 26%, which may be acceptable if your blended cost stays on target.

Note: Beverage cost is only one of the "big three." Labor and occupancy also need to fit. A low beverage cost does not guarantee profit if rent or labor is too high, so model the whole picture in the coffee shop profit calculator.

Checking Against the Market

Cost-based pricing gives you a floor; the market gives you a ceiling. Survey every competitor within walking distance and record prices for a drip coffee, a latte and a pastry. Then decide where you sit:

  • At market if your product and experience are comparable.
  • Above market if you offer clearly higher quality, a distinctive coffee program, better seating or exceptional speed, and your customers notice.
  • Below market rarely makes sense for an independent café; volume needed to compensate is hard to achieve, and raising prices later is harder than starting right.

If your cost-based price sits well above what the market accepts, look at recipe size, packaging cost and supplier pricing before cutting margin.

Pricing Psychology That Works in Cafés

Build a sensible size ladder

The extra cost of a larger size is mostly milk and a slightly bigger cup, so upsizing is usually high-margin. Price the increments so the larger size feels like good value, for example USD 0.50–0.75 per step, while still holding your beverage cost target.

Use anchors

Placing a premium item, such as a single-origin pour-over or a signature drink, near the top of the menu makes standard drinks feel reasonably priced by comparison.

Keep the menu board short and scannable

Group drinks by type, list sizes in a single row, and avoid dollar signs and long columns of numbers where local norms allow. A short menu speeds decisions, which speeds the line.

Price modifiers deliberately

Extra shots, syrups and alternative milks each have real costs. Cost them individually and price them clearly. Hidden or inconsistent modifier pricing frustrates customers and staff alike.

Round or charm prices?

Round prices (USD 5.00) speed cash handling and feel clean on a menu board; prices ending in .25, .50 or .75 give more room for fine-tuning. Pick one convention and apply it consistently.

Bundle to lift the average ticket

A coffee-and-pastry pairing at a small discount can increase the average ticket and move food that would otherwise be wasted at closing.

Keeping Prices Healthy Over Time

  • Re-cost after every supplier change. Coffee, dairy and packaging prices move; your spreadsheet should update in minutes.
  • Audit portioning monthly. Over-pouring milk by 30 ml per drink adds up across thousands of drinks.
  • Track waste daily. A simple log of discarded shots, milk and batch coffee shows whether your waste allowance is realistic.
  • Review the menu twice a year. Drop slow sellers that tie up inventory, and adjust prices in small steps rather than one big jump.
  • Account for taxes and fees. Sales tax treatment of food and drink, and whether prices are displayed tax-inclusive, varies by country and region; check with an accountant.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good beverage cost percentage for a coffee shop?

Many independent cafés aim for a blended beverage cost of roughly 20 to 30 percent of menu price, including cups and waste. Individual drinks vary: drip coffee and espresso usually sit low, while drinks with alternative milks, sauces or large sizes run higher.

How much does it cost to make a latte?

It depends on your coffee, milk and packaging prices. With illustrative wholesale prices, an 18 g double shot, about 280 ml of milk, a cup and lid and a small waste allowance might come to roughly USD 0.90 to 1.10, but you should cost it with your own supplier invoices.

Should I charge extra for oat or other plant-based milks?

Many cafés do, because plant-based milks often cost noticeably more per liter than dairy. Cost the drink both ways and set a surcharge that brings the plant-based version close to your target beverage cost.

How often should I review menu prices?

Recheck your drink costs whenever a supplier price changes and review the whole menu at least once or twice a year. Small, regular adjustments are usually easier for customers to accept than a single large increase.
