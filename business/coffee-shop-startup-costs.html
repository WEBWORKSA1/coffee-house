Coffee Shop Startup Costs: Line-by-Line Estimates

Startup budgets swing by hundreds of thousands of dollars depending on format and build-out. Here is a line-by-line framework with estimated ranges and the assumptions behind them.

8 min readBy the Coffee.House editorial teamUpdated October 2026How we write

"How much does it cost to open a coffee shop?" has no single answer, but it does have a structure. Every café budget is built from the same fifteen or so line items, and the format you choose determines which of them are small and which are enormous. This guide gives you that structure, estimated ranges for three common formats, and the levers that move each number. Use it to build your own budget, then replace every estimate with a real local quote before you commit.

Assumptions Behind These Estimates

All figures below are labeled estimates in US dollars, intended for early planning only. They assume:

  • A leased space (not purchased), in a mid-cost urban or suburban market.
  • A mix of new and quality used equipment, bought rather than leased.
  • Three formats: a cart or kiosk (no seating, short menu), a small café of roughly 50–110 m² (about 540–1,200 sq ft) with 10–30 seats and limited food, and a full-service café of roughly 140–280 m² (about 1,500–3,000 sq ft) with a kitchen and broader food menu.
  • The low end assumes a second-generation space or simple setup; the high end assumes a raw shell needing major plumbing, electrical and ventilation work.

Costs in high-rent cities, or in countries with different construction and labor markets, can fall well outside these ranges. Permit fees, import duties and taxes vary by jurisdiction; check with your local authorities and an accountant.

Notice how wide the ranges are. The single biggest variable is the condition of the space. A former café or restaurant with adequate drainage, a grease trap, three-phase or high-amperage power and ventilation can save tens of thousands of dollars compared with converting a retail shell.

What Drives Each Major Line Item

Build-out

Plumbing is the usual budget breaker: espresso machines, dishwashers, hand sinks and prep sinks each need water and drainage, and many health codes specify the number and type of sinks. Electrical upgrades are next, since espresso machines, ovens and refrigeration draw heavy loads. Add flooring, wall finishes, counters, lighting, accessible restrooms and any required ventilation. Always get at least two contractor bids, and add a 10–20% contingency.

Espresso equipment

A commercial two-group espresso machine plus two grinders (one for the house espresso, one for decaf or a second offering) is a common setup for a small café. Expected peak volume decides whether you need a three-group machine. See the coffee shop equipment checklist for a station-by-station list. Budget generously for grinders: they shape shot quality as much as the machine does.

Furniture and fit-out

Seating is where design ambitions inflate budgets. Decide how many seats your concept truly needs; a grab-and-go café may need only a ledge and a few stools, which also reduces the space you lease.

Working capital

This line is the one owners most often cut, and the one they most often regret cutting. A common rule is three to six months of fixed costs. For a small café with, say, USD 6,000 monthly rent, USD 14,000 base payroll and USD 2,000 in insurance, utilities and loan payments, that is USD 66,000–132,000 at the full rule, which is why many owners settle for the lower end and keep a line of credit available.

Ways to Lower Startup Costs Without Hurting the Business

  • Take a second-generation space. Existing drainage, power and ventilation are the biggest savings available.
  • Negotiate a tenant improvement allowance and rent-free fit-out period. Landlords often contribute when you sign a longer lease. Our location guide lists terms worth asking for.
  • Buy used where it is safe. Furniture, stainless tables, shelving and some refrigeration are good used purchases. Have used espresso machines inspected by a qualified technician.
  • Ask about roaster support. Some wholesale roasters offer equipment loans, financing referrals or training in exchange for a supply agreement. Read the terms carefully.
  • Shorten the menu. Fewer drinks and food items mean less equipment, inventory and training.
  • Phase the fit-out. Open with essentials and add the second grinder, extra seating or a patio once revenue proves demand.

Hidden and Easily Forgotten Costs

  • Utility connection fees and deposits.
  • Grease trap installation or upgrades, if required for food service.
  • Architect or designer fees, plus permit drawings.
  • Delivery, installation and commissioning of equipment.
  • Card processing fees, typically charged as a percentage of each transaction.
  • Software subscriptions for POS, scheduling, payroll and accounting.
  • Waste and recycling service contracts.
  • Recipe testing and staff practice coffee before opening, which can consume a surprising amount of milk and beans.

Turning the Estimate Into a Plan

A startup budget is one half of a financial model; the other half is your operating forecast. Carry these figures into your coffee shop business plan, where they become your funding request and your break-even analysis. If the total feels out of reach, revisit the format: a cart or kiosk can prove your concept and build a following with a fraction of the capital, as covered in the mobile coffee cart guide.

Whatever format you choose, replace each estimate with written quotes from local contractors, equipment dealers and insurers, and have an accountant review the plan. Regulations, fees and taxes vary by country and city, and local professionals will know the costs this guide cannot. Frequently asked questions

What is the biggest startup cost for a coffee shop?

For brick-and-mortar cafés it is usually the build-out: plumbing, electrical, ventilation, flooring, counters and finishes. For carts and kiosks, the cart or vehicle itself plus the espresso setup tends to dominate.

How much working capital should a new café keep in reserve?

A common planning rule is three to six months of fixed operating costs such as rent, base payroll, insurance and loan payments. New cafés often take several months to reach steady sales, so the reserve is what keeps you open during that ramp.

Is it cheaper to take over an existing café?

Often, yes. A second-generation café space with working plumbing, drainage, electrical capacity and ventilation can cut build-out costs substantially, though you should have the inherited equipment and systems inspected before agreeing on a price.

Are these cost figures accurate for my city?

They are broad estimates in US dollars based on stated assumptions, not quotes. Construction, rent, labor, permit fees and taxes vary widely by country and city, so get local contractor quotes and professional advice before committing.
