Coffee Shop Location: How to Choose and Score a Site

Your location sets the ceiling on what your café can earn, and the lease sets the floor on what it costs. Evaluate both with evidence, not instinct, using this framework and scoring matrix.

8 min readBy the Coffee.House editorial teamUpdated October 2026How we write

A great café in the wrong spot struggles; an average café in the right spot can do well. Location decisions feel intuitive, which is exactly why they go wrong. This guide gives you a structured way to evaluate sites: the criteria that matter, how to count foot traffic properly, how to test rent against realistic sales, which lease terms to push for, and a weighted scoring matrix that keeps you honest when comparing options.

Start With Your Concept

The right site depends on who you serve. A commuter espresso bar needs heavy morning foot traffic on the "going to work" side of the street. A neighborhood café with seating needs residents within walking distance and steady all-day visits. A destination specialty café can sit on a quieter street if the coffee draws people, but it needs parking or transit and a strong reputation. Write down your concept and peak daypart before viewing any space; our roadmap to opening a coffee shop explains how this decision shapes everything else.

Site Selection Criteria

  • Foot traffic at your peak hours. The single most important variable for most coffee concepts.
  • Visibility and frontage. Can people see the café, its signage and ideally the bar from the sidewalk or road?
  • Access. Transit stops, parking, bike racks, and whether drivers can pull in easily if you rely on car traffic.
  • Anchors and generators. Offices, schools, gyms, hospitals, transit hubs and grocery stores create repeat visits.
  • Competition. Nearby cafés are not always bad; they can prove demand. What matters is whether there is unmet demand you can capture.
  • Space suitability. Existing plumbing, drainage, electrical capacity, ventilation and restrooms dramatically affect build-out cost.
  • Zoning and permitted use. Confirm the space can legally operate as a food-service business. Rules vary by city; check with your local planning office.
  • Occupancy cost. Base rent plus shared charges, taxes and insurance passed through by the landlord.

How to Count Foot Traffic

  1. Pick the right windows. Count during your expected peak, such as 7:00–10:00 a.m. on weekdays, plus a midday and a weekend window.
  2. Count in 15-minute blocks. Use a tally counter or phone app, and record each block separately so you can see the shape of the rush.
  3. Repeat on at least three different days. Avoid holidays and unusual weather, or note them.
  4. Record direction and behavior. Note which side of the street people walk on, and how many already carry a coffee.
  5. Estimate capture. Only a small share of passers-by will become customers. Choose a cautious assumption and test what volume it produces.

Worked example (illustrative): you count an average of 420 people per 15 minutes between 7:00 and 9:00 a.m., or about 3,360 in two hours. At an assumed 3% capture rate, that is roughly 100 transactions in the morning peak. If your break-even requires 180 transactions a day, you need another 80 from the rest of the day. That is a reasonable target for some sites and a stretch for others, and now you can judge it with data.

Do the same exercise outside the nearest competing cafés. Count how many people enter during a busy 15-minute block and how long the queue gets. A competitor with a line out the door at 8:00 a.m. is evidence of demand that one bar cannot fully serve, while a quiet competitor on a busy street may signal that the traffic is passing through rather than stopping. Neither observation is proof on its own, but together with your counts they make your sales forecast far more defensible to a lender, a landlord and yourself.

Testing Rent Against Sales

A widely used guideline is to keep total occupancy cost at roughly 8–12% of projected sales. Work backward from conservative revenue:

If a space asks USD 7,500 per month all-in and your conservative forecast is USD 45,000 in sales, occupancy would be about 17% of sales, well above the guideline. Lease Terms to Negotiate

Commercial leases are negotiable, especially on spaces that have sat vacant. Ask a lawyer experienced in commercial leases in your jurisdiction to review any offer before signing. Terms worth discussing include:

  • Rent-free fit-out period. Time to build out before rent starts, often linked to the scale of works.
  • Tenant improvement allowance. A landlord contribution toward construction, sometimes in exchange for a longer term.
  • Term and renewal options. A shorter initial term with options to renew balances risk with security.
  • Exclusivity. The landlord agrees not to lease nearby units in the same property to another café.
  • Rent escalations. Understand exactly how and when rent increases.
  • Shared charges. Clarify which costs are passed through and whether they are capped.
  • Repair responsibilities. Who pays for HVAC, roof, plumbing, grease traps and structural issues?
  • Use clause. Make it broad enough to cover food, beverages and retail.
  • Signage rights. Exterior signage, window graphics and sidewalk boards.
  • Assignment and subletting. Your ability to sell the business with the lease if you exit.
  • Personal guarantee. Try to limit its amount or duration.
  • Contingencies. Make the lease conditional on obtaining required permits where possible.

A Weighted Site Scoring Matrix

Score each site from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent) on each criterion. Multiply by the weight and divide by 5 to get weighted points; the maximum total is 100. Adjust weights to suit your concept. The scores below are an illustrative comparison of three hypothetical sites.

Notice that Site A has the best foot traffic but loses to Site B, which is cheaper to occupy and to build out. That is the kind of trade-off a matrix makes visible. If two sites score within a few points, go back and count traffic again; foot traffic is the criterion most worth extra data.

Red Flags and Final Checks

  • Several food businesses have failed in the same unit in recent years. Find out why.
  • Upcoming construction, road changes or an anchor tenant leaving.
  • No realistic route for a grease trap, exhaust or extra drainage if your menu needs them.
  • Restrictions on hours, signage or outdoor seating that undermine your concept.
  • A landlord unwilling to put verbal promises in writing.

Before signing, have a contractor estimate the build-out so you can add it to the startup cost budget, and confirm permitted use with local authorities. If you have a shortlist and want an outside opinion, our free launch consultation can help you pressure-test the numbers.

Frequently asked questions

How much rent can a coffee shop afford?

A common rule of thumb is to keep total occupancy cost, including base rent and shared charges, at roughly 8 to 12 percent of projected sales. Calculate it from conservative sales forecasts, not optimistic ones.

How do I measure foot traffic for a café site?

Stand at the site with a tally counter during the hours that matter for your concept, typically weekday mornings, and count passers-by in 15-minute blocks over several different days. Note direction of travel and how many people are already carrying coffee.

Is a corner location better for a coffee shop?

Corners often offer better visibility and two streams of foot traffic, but they usually cost more. Score a corner against other sites on the same criteria rather than assuming it wins.

What is an exclusivity clause in a café lease?

It is a lease term in which the landlord agrees not to lease other units in the same property to a competing business, such as another café. It can be valuable in shopping centers and mixed-use buildings.
