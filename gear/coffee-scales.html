Coffee Scales: How to Choose One for Brewing

Weighing coffee and water is the fastest way to consistent brewing. Here is what makes a scale good for coffee, and which features are worth paying for.

7 min readBy the Coffee.House editorial teamUpdated October 2026How we write

A scale may be the least glamorous piece of coffee gear, but it is one of the most important. Brewing is a recipe, and recipes depend on ratios. A tablespoon of light-roast beans can weigh noticeably more than the same scoop of dark roast, and water poured "to the line" varies every time. Weighing removes the guesswork, lets you repeat a great cup, and makes it possible to troubleshoot when something goes wrong.

Why weigh your coffee

Weighing allows you to use ratios such as 1:16 for pour-over or 1:2 for espresso, which you can scale up or down precisely with our brew ratio calculator. It also helps you track how fast you pour, how much espresso you pull, and whether a change in taste came from the coffee or from your measurements. Once you weigh, you can copy recipes from guides like our V60 pour-over guide and get comparable results.

Jeweler or pocket scale0.01 g100–500 gDosing beans preciselyLow capacity; not for brewing water

Most home brewers are best served by a dedicated coffee scale with 0.1 g resolution and around 2 kg capacity. If you make espresso, check whether it fits on your machine's drip tray.

Buying criteria that matter

Resolution and accuracy

Resolution is the smallest increment the display shows; accuracy is how close the reading is to the true weight. A scale can show 0.1 g and still be inaccurate. Look for consistent readings: place the same object on the scale several times and check that the result is stable.

Response time

When you pour water, a slow scale lags behind, so you overshoot. A fast-responding scale updates in a fraction of a second, which matters for pour-over and espresso where you stop at a specific weight.

Capacity

Add the weight of your brewer, carafe and water. A ceramic dripper and glass server plus 500 g of water can approach 1 kg. Choose at least 2 kg for pour-over; espresso scales can be smaller.

Built-in timer

A timer on the scale display lets you track weight and time together, which is invaluable for pour-over pacing and espresso shot timing. Some scales start the timer automatically when flow begins.

Size and footprint

For espresso, measure your drip tray and the space under the portafilter. For pour-over, make sure the platform is large enough to hold your carafe stably.

Water resistance and power

Coffee scales get splashed. Some resistance to spills helps them survive. Rechargeable scales, often via USB-C, avoid buying batteries; replaceable batteries mean the scale is never stuck charging. Check for an auto-off setting you can disable, since a scale turning off mid-brew is frustrating.

How a scale improves each brew method

  • Pour-over: lets you hit bloom and pulse targets, such as 45 g for a 30-second bloom, then pour to a final weight at a steady pace.
  • Espresso: measures both the dose in the basket, often 16–20 g, and the liquid yield in the cup, so you can control the brew ratio rather than relying on shot volume, which crema distorts. See our espresso at home guide.
  • Immersion methods: French press and cold brew are forgiving, but weighing still keeps strength consistent from batch to batch.
  • Milk drinks: weighing milk in the pitcher reduces waste and helps you steam the same amount every time.

Smart features: useful or not?

  • Auto-tare: zeroes the scale when a vessel is placed on it. Convenient, occasionally annoying.
  • Auto-start timer: useful for espresso, where your hands are busy.
  • Flow-rate display: shows grams per second; helpful for learning consistent pours.
  • App connectivity: logs brews and graphs extraction. Fun for enthusiasts, but unnecessary for great coffee.

What matters vs marketing fluff

Fast, stable readings and a built-in timer matter most. A platform that fits your setup matters next. Beyond that, extra modes and app integrations are conveniences rather than necessities. Be cautious of claims of 0.01 g precision on scales meant for brewing; at high capacities, that precision is rarely real or useful.

Pro tip: Place a folded towel or silicone mat under the vessel rather than under the scale. It protects the platform from heat without dampening readings.

Using and caring for your scale

  1. Tare everything. Place your brewer and filter on the scale, then zero before adding coffee.
  2. Weigh beans before grinding. Dose whole beans for accuracy, then check the ground weight to spot grinder retention.
  3. Pour to target weights. Follow the recipe's water weights and times.
  4. Check accuracy. Every few months, test with a calibration weight.
  5. Clean gently. Wipe with a damp cloth; never submerge or run under a tap.

A scale is also central to a well-organized workstation. See our home coffee bar setup guide for how it fits into a smooth workflow.

Frequently asked questions

Why weigh coffee instead of using scoops?

Coffee beans vary in size and density, especially between roasts, so a scoop can hold noticeably different amounts. Weighing in grams gives the same dose every time.

Can I use a kitchen scale for coffee?

A kitchen scale with 1 g resolution works for French press and cold brew. For pour-over and espresso, a scale with 0.1 g resolution and faster response is much more useful.

What resolution do I need for espresso?

A 0.1 g resolution is standard and sufficient for espresso. Finer 0.01 g scales are rarely necessary and often have low capacity.

Do coffee scales need calibrating?

Occasionally. Many scales can be calibrated with a known weight. Check accuracy periodically using a calibration weight or an item of known mass.
