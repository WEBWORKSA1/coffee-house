Latte Recipe: How to Make a Café-Style Latte at Home

The latte is espresso softened with plenty of steamed milk and a thin cap of foam. It is the gentlest classic espresso drink and the ideal canvas for latte art.

4 min readBy the Coffee.House editorial teamUpdated October 2026How we write
Yield1 drink (about 260 ml)
Time5 min
DifficultyMedium
1×

Ingredients

  • 18 g finely ground espresso coffee (for a double shot, about 36 g liquid espresso)
  • 200 ml cold whole milk
  • Sugar or syrup to taste (optional)

Method

  1. Preheat a 300–350 ml cup with hot water.
  2. Pull a double espresso of about 36 g in 25–32 seconds directly into the emptied, warm cup.
  3. Pour the cold milk into a steaming jug to just below the base of the spout.
  4. Purge the steam wand, then stretch the milk briefly with the tip just below the surface until it has grown by about 10–15 percent.
  5. Submerge the tip slightly to create a whirlpool and heat to 60–65°C, when the jug is too hot to hold comfortably.
  6. Tap the jug on the counter and swirl until the milk looks glossy like wet paint.
  7. Pour the milk into the espresso from a height of about 10 cm, then lower the jug close to the surface to finish with a thin layer of foam.

A latte is the most approachable of the classic espresso drinks: one double shot, a generous amount of steamed milk and a thin, velvety layer of foam on top. It is milky, sweet and comforting, and because the milk is so smooth it is the drink most baristas use to practice latte art.

What it is and where it comes from

The word comes from the Italian caffè latte, simply "milk coffee." Milk and coffee have been combined at home across Europe for centuries, but the tall, foam-capped espresso drink most cafés serve today is generally considered a later development that spread widely through North American and international café culture in the late twentieth century. In Italy, ordering just "latte" will often get you a glass of milk.

Flavor and texture

With roughly five parts milk to one part espresso, the coffee reads as a warm, toasty background rather than the main event. Steamed milk tastes sweeter than cold milk because heating it to around 60°C brings out its natural lactose sweetness. The ideal texture is uniform and glossy with no visible bubbles, and the foam layer is thin, about 1 cm. If you want more coffee intensity in a smaller cup, try a flat white instead.

Technique notes

Good lattes start with good espresso; see our espresso at home guide to dial in your shot. For the milk, use it cold from the fridge so you have more time to texture it. Stretch, meaning add air, only during the first few seconds while the milk is still cool, listening for a gentle tearing or hissing sound rather than loud screeching. Then sink the wand to spin the milk until it reaches temperature. Pour soon after steaming, before the foam separates from the liquid.

Variations

  • Flavored latte: add 10–15 ml of vanilla, caramel or hazelnut syrup to the espresso before pouring the milk.
  • Oat or other plant milk latte: use a barista-style plant milk, and steam it slightly cooler, around 55–60°C.
  • Iced version: see our iced latte recipe, which uses cold milk and ice instead of steam.
  • Stovetop latte: use about 60 ml of strong moka pot coffee in place of espresso.

Common mistakes

Overheating the milk past 70°C makes it taste flat and slightly cooked. Adding too much air creates stiff, dry foam that sits on top rather than blending in. Letting the espresso sit while you steam lets its crema fade, so steam first if your machine allows, or work quickly.

Serving tips

Serve in a warmed ceramic cup or glass of 300–350 ml. If you are learning latte art, start with a simple heart: pour high to blend, then drop the spout low and close to the surface, let a white circle form, and finish with a quick line through the middle. Our latte art basics article goes further.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a latte and a cappuccino?

A latte has more milk and a thinner layer of foam, about 1 cm, so it tastes milder and creamier. A cappuccino is smaller with a thicker, airier foam cap and a stronger coffee flavor.

What milk is best for a latte?

Whole cow's milk steams most easily into glossy microfoam thanks to its balance of fat and protein. Among plant milks, barista-style oat milk usually performs best.

Can I make a latte without an espresso machine?

Yes, approximately. Use a strong concentrate from a moka pot or AeroPress and froth warm milk with a French press plunger or handheld frother. The texture and intensity will be lighter than café espresso.
