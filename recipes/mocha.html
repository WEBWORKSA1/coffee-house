Mocha Recipe: Espresso, Chocolate and Steamed Milk

A mocha is a latte with chocolate. Made with a simple homemade sauce or real dark chocolate, it is rich and dessert-like without the cloying sweetness of syrups.

4 min readBy the Coffee.House editorial teamUpdated October 2026How we write
Yield1 drink (about 280 ml)
Time6 min
DifficultyEasy
1×

Ingredients

  • 18 g finely ground espresso coffee (double shot, about 36 g liquid)
  • 10 g unsweetened cocoa powder
  • 10–15 g sugar
  • 15 ml hot water
  • 200 ml cold whole milk
  • Whipped cream or grated chocolate to garnish (optional)

Method

  1. Preheat a 300–350 ml cup.
  2. Whisk the cocoa, sugar and hot water in the cup into a smooth, glossy paste.
  3. Pull a double espresso directly onto the chocolate paste and stir until fully combined.
  4. Steam the milk to 60–65°C, adding a modest amount of air for a thin foam layer.
  5. Pour the milk into the chocolate espresso, stirring briefly to blend.
  6. Top with whipped cream or grated chocolate if desired.

A mocha brings together two of the most popular warm drinks, coffee and hot chocolate, in one cup. Built on espresso and steamed milk like a latte, it adds a chocolate base that turns it rich, round and gently sweet.

What it is and where it comes from

The name traces back to Mocha, a port city on the Red Sea coast of Yemen that was central to the early coffee trade. Over time, "mocha" came to describe coffee combined with chocolate, possibly because some coffees from that region were described as chocolatey. The modern café mocha, an espresso, chocolate and steamed milk drink, became widespread with the growth of espresso bars. You can read more about the trade routes in our history of coffee article.

Flavor and texture

A well-made mocha tastes like coffee first and chocolate second, with the cocoa deepening the espresso's roasted notes. Homemade cocoa sauce gives a darker, less sugary flavor than commercial syrups. Melted dark chocolate, around 60–70 percent cocoa, adds a thicker, silkier body. The milk should be smooth with only a thin cap of foam so the drink feels luxurious rather than airy.

Technique notes

Always make the chocolate into a paste before adding milk; dry cocoa added to milk will form stubborn lumps. Pulling the espresso directly onto the paste helps dissolve it completely. If you use chopped dark chocolate, melt about 20 g into the hot espresso and stir for 30 seconds. Medium to medium-dark roasts with nutty or cocoa notes, such as many Brazilian coffees, pair especially well. Steam the milk the way you would for a latte.

Variations

  • White chocolate mocha: melt 20 g of white chocolate into the espresso; reduce or skip the sugar.
  • Mexican-style mocha: add a pinch of cinnamon and a tiny pinch of chili to the cocoa paste.
  • Mint mocha: add a drop of peppermint extract to the chocolate.
  • Iced mocha: dissolve the paste in hot espresso, then pour over ice with cold milk.
  • No-machine mocha: use strong AeroPress or moka pot coffee in place of espresso.

Common mistakes

Too much chocolate or sugar overwhelms the coffee and turns the drink into flavored hot chocolate. Lumps from undissolved cocoa are the second most common problem. Heavy whipped cream on top can also chill the drink quickly, so add it just before serving.

Serving tips

Serve in a warmed 300–350 ml mug. A sprinkle of flaky salt over the whipped cream or a few shavings of dark chocolate make it feel special. Adjust sweetness to your beans: brighter coffees need a little more sugar to balance their acidity.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a mocha and a hot chocolate?

A mocha contains espresso as well as chocolate and steamed milk, so it has a coffee flavor and caffeine. Hot chocolate has no coffee.

Can I use cocoa powder instead of chocolate sauce?

Yes. Mix unsweetened cocoa with sugar and a little hot water or espresso into a smooth paste before adding milk, or it will clump.

Why is it called a mocha?

The name is generally linked to the port of Mocha in Yemen, once a major coffee trading hub. Some coffees from the region have chocolatey notes, and the word later came to mean coffee with chocolate.
