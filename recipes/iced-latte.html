Iced Latte Recipe: Cold, Creamy and Never Watery

An iced latte is espresso and cold milk over ice. It is simple, but the order you build it in and the strength of your shot decide whether it is bold or watery.

3 min readBy the Coffee.House editorial teamUpdated October 2026How we write
Yield1 drink (about 350 ml with ice)
Time4 min
DifficultyEasy
1×

Ingredients

  • 18 g finely ground espresso coffee (double shot, about 36 g liquid)
  • 180 ml cold whole milk
  • 1 cup of ice (about 150 g)
  • 10–20 ml simple syrup (optional)

Method

  1. Fill a 400 ml glass with ice.
  2. Add simple syrup to the glass if using.
  3. Pour the cold milk over the ice, leaving room at the top.
  4. Pull a double espresso into a small jug or cup.
  5. Pour the espresso slowly over the milk to create a layered effect.
  6. Stir before drinking.

An iced latte is the warm-weather sibling of the classic latte: espresso and cold milk over ice, with no steaming required. It is quick, creamy and refreshing, and it works just as well with a home espresso machine as with cold brew concentrate.

What it is and where it comes from

Cold coffee and milk drinks have been made for a long time in many countries, but the iced latte as a standard café menu item grew alongside espresso bar culture and the rising popularity of iced coffee drinks in recent decades. It is now one of the best-selling cold drinks on many café menus, especially in summer.

Flavor and texture

Cold milk tastes less sweet than steamed milk, so an iced latte reads as cleaner and more refreshing, with the espresso more prominent. There is no foam, just a smooth, chilled body. Bright, fruity espressos can taste like coffee and cream; chocolatey espressos taste like a rich chocolate milk with a coffee finish.

Technique notes

Build the drink in the right order: ice, sweetener, milk, then espresso. Hot espresso poured straight onto ice melts it quickly and thins the drink, while milk acts as a buffer. Use a slightly stronger, shorter shot than usual, or even a triple, because cold temperatures mute flavor. Use plenty of ice so the drink is truly cold; a half-filled glass melts faster. If you do not have espresso, our cold brew concentrate is an excellent substitute, and our cold brew vs iced coffee article explains the taste differences.

Variations

  • Coffee ice cubes: freeze leftover coffee in a tray so the drink gets stronger as it melts.
  • Shaken iced latte: shake espresso, syrup and ice in a jar for 10 seconds, strain, then top with milk for a frothy texture.
  • Vanilla or caramel: replace simple syrup with 15 ml of flavored syrup.
  • Cold foam top: froth 50 ml of cold skim or low-fat milk with a handheld frother and spoon it on top.
  • Oat iced latte: oat milk adds natural sweetness and a creamy body.

Common mistakes

Using a weak single shot is the most common error; the drink tastes like cold milk. Adding granulated sugar leaves gritty crystals at the bottom. Letting the drink sit too long also dilutes it, so build it right before serving.

Serving tips

Serve in a tall clear glass to show off the layers, with a reusable straw or a long spoon for stirring. To make simple syrup, dissolve equal weights of sugar and hot water and refrigerate for up to a few weeks. For a sweeter, richer iced drink, try our Vietnamese iced coffee.

Frequently asked questions

Should I pour espresso over ice or milk first?

Pour milk over ice first, then add the espresso. The milk buffers the hot shot so it melts less ice and the drink stays stronger, and you get an attractive layered look.

How do I sweeten an iced latte?

Use a liquid sweetener such as simple syrup, which mixes instantly into cold drinks. Granulated sugar does not dissolve well in cold milk.

Can I make an iced latte without espresso?

Yes. Use about 60 ml of cold brew concentrate or strong, cooled AeroPress or moka pot coffee in place of the espresso.
