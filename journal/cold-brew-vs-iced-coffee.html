Cold Brew vs Iced Coffee: Differences Explained

Both are cold, both are coffee, but they are brewed in completely different ways. Here is how cold brew and iced coffee compare in flavor, strength, caffeine and convenience.

6 min readBy the Coffee.House editorial teamUpdated October 2026How we write

Walk into almost any café in summer and you will see both cold brew and iced coffee on the menu, often at different prices. They are not the same drink with different names. One is brewed with cold water over many hours; the other is brewed hot and chilled quickly. That single difference in temperature changes which flavor compounds end up in the glass, how strong the coffee is, how long it keeps and how much planning it takes.

The Core Difference

Cold brew is made by steeping coarsely ground coffee in cold or room-temperature water for 12–24 hours, then filtering it. Heat never enters the process. The result is usually a concentrate that is diluted before serving.

Iced coffee is coffee brewed hot and then cooled. The best version, often called flash brew or Japanese-style iced coffee, brews hot coffee directly onto ice so it chills within seconds. Lesser versions simply let brewed coffee cool, or pour it over a glass of ice where it dilutes unevenly.

Water temperatureCold or room temperatureAbout 92–96°C (198–205°F)
Brew time12–24 hours3–5 minutes
GrindCoarseMedium to medium-fine
Typical ratio1:4–1:8 concentrate, then dilutedAbout 1:15–1:16 total, including ice
FlavorSmooth, chocolatey, low perceived acidityBright, aromatic, crisp
Shelf lifeAbout 1–2 weeks refrigeratedBest the same day

Why Temperature Changes Flavor

Hot water is an aggressive solvent. It quickly dissolves acids, sugars, aromatic oils and bitter compounds. Cold water is far gentler and slower, and it pulls some compounds much less efficiently than others. Many of the bright, fruity acids and volatile aromatics that make hot coffee lively are extracted in smaller amounts, and so are some of the harsher bitter notes.

That is why cold brew tastes rounder, heavier and more chocolatey, with a soft finish. It is also why cold brew can taste a little flat if you use a delicate, floral coffee: the qualities that make that coffee special are the ones cold extraction tends to mute. Many people describe cold brew as "less acidic," and it does taste that way, but measured pH differences are often small. The change is mainly in perceived sourness and bitterness.

Flash brew keeps the hot-water extraction, with all its aromatic complexity, then traps it by chilling immediately. Cooling fast helps preserve volatile aromas that would otherwise drift off as coffee sits warm. It is the better choice when you want an iced cup that tastes recognizably like the origin on the bag.

How to Make Each at Home

Cold brew concentrate

  1. Grind coarse. Aim for coarse sea salt; see our grind size chart.
  2. Combine. Mix 100 g coffee with 700 ml (about 24 fl oz) of cold, filtered water, a 1:7 ratio. Stir until all grounds are wet.
  3. Steep. Cover and leave for 14–18 hours at room temperature, or 18–24 hours in the refrigerator.
  4. Filter. Strain through a fine mesh, then through a paper filter for a cleaner cup.
  5. Dilute to serve. Start with one part concentrate to one part water or milk over ice, and adjust.

For a fuller walkthrough with troubleshooting, see our complete cold brew guide.

Flash brew iced coffee

  1. Weigh ice. Put 150 g of ice in your carafe or server.
  2. Adjust the water. For 25 g of coffee, use 250 g of hot water instead of the usual 400 g, since the ice provides the rest. The total liquid stays at a ratio of about 1:16.
  3. Grind a touch finer. Less hot water means less extraction, so go one step finer than you would for hot pour-over.
  4. Brew directly onto the ice. Use your normal pour-over technique. Swirl the carafe at the end until most ice has melted.
  5. Serve over fresh ice. Pour into a glass with new ice so it stays cold without further dilution.

Caffeine Compared

Cold brew concentrate is strong, and undiluted it can contain well over 200 mg of caffeine per 240 ml (8 fl oz) serving. Once diluted about 1:1, it typically falls into roughly the same range as hot brewed coffee, commonly 100–200 mg per glass. Flash brew iced coffee usually lands at around 80–150 mg per serving, because the ice takes up part of the volume.

The real swing factor is how much concentrate you pour and how big the glass is. Large café cold brews can be surprisingly high in caffeine. Our caffeine guide lists typical ranges for other drinks too.

Cost, Convenience and Shelf Life

Cold brew uses more coffee per cup, often twice as much as hot brewing, because cold extraction is less efficient. In exchange, one batch gives you a week or more of ready-to-pour coffee, which is why cafés like it: it is consistent and fast to serve during a rush. Keep concentrate sealed and cold, and taste it after a week; it gradually loses brightness and can pick up refrigerator odors.

Flash brew uses an ordinary amount of coffee and takes minutes, but you have to brew each time. It is best drunk fresh, within a few hours.

Choosing Beans for Each

  • For cold brew: medium to medium-dark roasts with chocolate, nut and caramel notes shine. Brazilian, Colombian and many Central American coffees are dependable.
  • For flash brew: lighter roasts with fruit and floral notes work beautifully. Washed Ethiopian and Kenyan coffees taste bright and juicy over ice.
  • For milk-based iced drinks: cold brew concentrate holds its own against milk; see our iced latte for an espresso-based alternative.

Pro tip: Freeze leftover cold brew or flash brew in ice cube trays. Coffee ice cubes chill your next glass without watering it down.

Which Should You Choose?

Choose cold brew if you want a smooth, mellow, low-bitterness drink that is ready whenever you are, especially with milk. Choose flash brew iced coffee if you want vivid flavor, aroma and clarity, and you do not mind brewing to order. Many coffee lovers keep cold brew in the fridge for weekdays and save flash brew for weekend single-origins. Both are worth learning, and once you know how they work, you can tune either one to your taste.

Frequently asked questions

Is cold brew stronger than iced coffee?

Cold brew concentrate is usually much stronger, but it is normally diluted before drinking. Once diluted, a glass of cold brew and a glass of iced coffee can land in similar caffeine ranges, depending on the recipe.

Is cold brew less acidic?

Cold brew generally tastes less acidic and less bitter because cold water extracts fewer of the compounds that create sharp, bright flavors. Measured pH differences are often modest, so the difference is mostly in perceived taste.

Can I just put hot coffee in the fridge to make iced coffee?

You can, but it tends to taste flat and stale because aromatics fade and the coffee oxidizes as it cools slowly. Brewing hot directly onto ice locks in more aroma.

How long does cold brew last?

Cold brew concentrate typically keeps well for about one to two weeks in a sealed container in the refrigerator, though flavor is best in the first several days. Iced coffee is best drunk the day it is made.
